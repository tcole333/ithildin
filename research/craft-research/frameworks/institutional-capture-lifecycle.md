## Definition

Institutional Capture Lifecycle describes the temporal evolution by which a private entity progressively colonizes a regulatory or enforcement institution, moving through distinct phases from individual influence to structural capture to explicit alignment. Static models of regulatory capture (Stigler) describe the equilibrium state. This lens describes the *process* -- the phase transitions that make capture durable and eventually self-sustaining.

The lifecycle is distinct from the Private Order model, which describes the access-controlled network as a steady state. The Institutional Capture Lifecycle describes how that state is *built over time* through a sequence of moves that each appear individually reasonable but collectively produce institutional colonization. No single career move (prosecutor joins defense firm, defense lawyer becomes AG) is remarkable. The lifecycle becomes visible only when you track the full sequence across decades and see how each move reinforces the next.

In this investigation: The Kirkland & Ellis / DOJ thread provides the clearest multi-decade case. Phase 1 (2008-2012): Mark Filip writes DPA guidelines as Deputy AG, leaves government, joins K&E, and deploys those same guidelines defending BP for $4.5B. This is standard revolving door -- individual actors moving between regulator and regulated. Phase 2 (2015-2021): The Filip-Benczkowski team handles VW ($4.3B) and Boeing ($2.5B), with prosecutors routinely joining K&E after settlements. The firm has now *institutionalized* the revolving door as a talent pipeline. Phase 3 (2017-2020): Ten K&E alumni simultaneously occupy top DOJ positions under AG Barr, creating a critical mass where the regulated entity and the regulator share the same professional culture, referral networks, and career expectations. This is capture -- not individual influence but structural equivalence. Phase 4 (2025): K&E pledges $125M in pro bono work to the Trump administration while alumni hold DOJ positions. The relationship is no longer hidden or informal; it is formalized institutional alignment.

Deutsche Bank's compliance structure shows a parallel lifecycle in miniature. Phase 1: Revenue-generating executives (EXECUTIVE-1) informally approve client relationships, with compliance ratifying after the fact. Phase 2: The ARRC (risk committee) is structured to include revenue-side personnel who frame risk decisions. Phase 3: Compliance officers interpret monitoring conditions in ways that effectively define away suspicious activity ("normal for this client"). Phase 4: The entire monitoring apparatus operates as a permission-granting mechanism for the revenue relationship.

## Phases

1. **Individual Access** (revolving door) -- Individual actors move between the regulator and the regulated entity. Each move is individually unremarkable. The private entity accumulates institutional knowledge of the regulator's decision-making processes, priorities, and personnel.

2. **Institutional Pipeline** (talent capture) -- The movement becomes systematic rather than individual. The private entity recruits a *class* of regulators, not just individuals. The regulator begins to view the private entity as a natural career destination. Recruitment flows shape who applies to the regulator in the first place -- people who plan to rotate to the private sector later.

3. **Structural Capture** (cultural colonization) -- A critical mass of former regulators now works for the private entity, and a critical mass of former private entity employees now works at the regulator. The two institutions share professional culture, social networks, and cognitive frameworks. Decisions at the regulator are not corrupted by external pressure but shaped by internal norms that were imported from the regulated industry.

4. **Formalized Alignment** (institutional partnership) -- The relationship between regulator and regulated entity becomes explicit and public. Pro bono commitments, secondment programs, joint task forces, shared advisory boards. The capture is no longer hidden because it no longer needs to be -- it has become the institutional norm.

## Detection Markers

- Mapping of career paths between a specific private entity and a specific regulator over 10+ years (pattern of the *same* firm, not generic industry movement)
- Individual who drafts policy/rules while at regulator, then deploys those rules on behalf of clients at the private entity
- 3+ alumni of the same firm simultaneously holding senior positions at the regulator
- Consent orders, DPAs, or settlement terms that closely track the private entity's standard advocacy positions (the regulator adopts the regulated entity's framing)
- Compliance/oversight functions reporting to or controlled by revenue-generating personnel rather than independent governance
- Risk committee conditions imposed but not communicated to operational staff (governance as ceremony, not control)
- Formal partnership announcements, pro bono commitments, or secondment programs between regulator and regulated entity

## Why Existing Models Miss This

- **Private Order** describes the network as a static structure; this lens describes how the structure is built over time through identifiable phases
- **Enabler Gradient** describes degrees of individual complicity; this lens describes institutional transformation where individual actors are following professional norms
- **Regulatory capture** (Tier 3 reference, Stigler) describes the economic equilibrium of capture but not the temporal mechanics of how it develops through specific phases
- **Normalization of deviance** (Tier 3 reference, Vaughan) describes institutional drift but not the deliberate (if emergent) colonization of one institution by another

## Transferability

The four-phase lifecycle appears in: Big Four accounting firms and financial regulators (PCAOB, SEC); pharmaceutical companies and FDA (advisory committee capture); military contractors and DOD (revolving door to institutional pipeline to structural integration); tech companies and FTC/DOJ Antitrust (emerging Phase 2-3). The diagnostic question for any regulator-regulated pair is: which phase are they in? Phase 1 (individual revolving door) is common and sometimes healthy. Phase 3-4 (structural capture to formalized alignment) represents a qualitative transformation in institutional independence.

## Limitations

- The phases are ideal types. Real-world capture may skip phases, overlap them, or regress. Not all revolving door activity leads to structural capture -- the model requires evidence of progression through phases, not just Phase 1 activity.
- Distinguishing "shared professional culture" (Phase 3) from simple "industry expertise" is difficult. Regulators reasonably hire people with industry knowledge. The diagnostic is whether the expertise serves the regulator's mission or the industry's interests -- and this is often ambiguous.
- The model works best when applied to a specific firm-regulator pair over a decade+. Applying it to short timeframes or broad industry categories produces false positives.
- Attributing *intent* to the lifecycle is usually wrong. Phase transitions emerge from individual career incentives, not organizational strategy. K&E didn't "plan" to capture the DOJ; thousands of individually rational career decisions produced the outcome.
