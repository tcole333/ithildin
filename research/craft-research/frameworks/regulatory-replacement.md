## Definition

Regulatory Replacement extends Stigler's theory of regulatory capture to describe a more extreme form: rather than *influencing* the regulator through lobbying, information asymmetry, and revolving doors, the regulated interest *becomes* the regulator by placing its own people in charge of the agency. You don't capture the agency — you ARE the agency.

Classical regulatory capture (Stigler) assumes the regulatory institution maintains its formal structure and mission while being gradually co-opted by the industry it regulates. The mechanisms are indirect: industry provides better information than public interest groups, career incentives pull regulators toward industry perspectives, lobbying shapes the regulatory agenda. The institution persists; its outputs shift.

Regulatory Replacement short-circuits this process. Instead of gradually influencing the institution over years, you place personnel from the regulated industry directly into leadership positions and simultaneously defund, reorganize, or structurally weaken the institution's capacity for independent action. The regulatory institution may continue to exist formally, but its operational character transforms from independent oversight to industry self-governance with the legitimacy of state authority.

Having the classical capture model as a baseline is analytically useful because it allows you to distinguish what you're observing. When someone says "this is just the revolving door," you can say: no, the revolving door is Stigler's capture — industry veterans moving into regulation and back. What you're documenting is structurally different: the agency's leadership, operational capacity, and mission have been replaced, not merely influenced.

## Mechanism

1. **Leadership placement** — Install industry figures in agency leadership (connects to Personnel Pipeline). Not career regulators who are sympathetic to industry, but direct imports from the regulated sector.
2. **Capacity reduction** — Defund investigative and enforcement divisions. Reduce staff through hiring freezes, reorganization, or mass termination. Eliminate institutional knowledge by removing career employees.
3. **Mission redefinition** — Shift the agency's operational focus from enforcement/oversight to "facilitation," "partnership," or "efficiency." The language of Depoliticization covers the mission change.
4. **Process modification** — Change enforcement priorities, investigation triggers, penalty structures, and reporting requirements to reduce friction for the industry the agency nominally oversees.
5. **Institutional hollowing** — The agency retains its formal name, its statutory authority, and its organizational chart, but its operational capacity to perform independent oversight has been eliminated. The shell persists; the function is gone.

## Detection Markers

- Agency leadership drawn directly from regulated industry without meaningful cooling-off period
- Simultaneous leadership change AND structural/budget reduction (capture alone doesn't require gutting the agency)
- Enforcement action rates dropping sharply after personnel change (not gradually, which suggests capture; sharply, which suggests replacement)
- Career staff departures clustering after new leadership arrives
- Mission statement or strategic plan changes that reframe the agency's role from "oversight" to "partnership" or "facilitation"
- Compare to the Stigler baseline: does this look like gradual influence (capture) or structural transformation (replacement)?

## Limitations

- Political appointees who come from industry are a normal feature of democratic governance. The model applies when industry placement is combined with capacity reduction and mission transformation — not merely when a new administration appoints people with industry background.
- Distinguishing capture from replacement requires a baseline understanding of the agency's prior operational capacity. Without that baseline, any change looks like replacement.
