# Cluster F: Structural Narrative in Longform Nonfiction

*Research report for Ithildin OSINT Investigation Platform — article generation system design*

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Structural narrative is the discipline of using the *arrangement* of material — not just the material itself — to make an argument. The writers studied in this cluster share a conviction that structure in nonfiction operates like a skeleton: readers should never see it, but without it, the body collapses.

**Key principles for Ithildin article generation:**

1. **Structure IS argument.** McPhee's non-chronological arrangements force readers to perceive connections that chronological order obscures. Keefe's character-web structure argues that systemic failure cannot be reduced to individual blame. The choice of structure is the first editorial decision and the most consequential one.

2. **The dual-spine technique.** McPhee's Interstate 80 in *Annals of the Former World* provides spatial orientation while the geological content provides temporal depth. Ithildin articles need an equivalent: one spine the reader can hold onto (a person, a timeline, a transaction chain) while the investigative content spirals outward into systemic complexity.

3. **Portrait-as-system.** Every writer in this cluster uses individuals as entry points into systems. McPhee profiles a ship captain to explain maritime commerce. Caro profiles Robert Moses to explain how power works in American cities. Keefe profiles the Sacklers to autopsy pharmaceutical regulation. The person is the door; the system is the room. Ithildin dossiers should work this way: the target IS the entry point, but the network is the subject.

4. **The evidence budget.** Every one of these writers has 10x more material than they use. McPhee's principle — "Writing is selection" — means that a target with 200+ findings cannot have all of them in an article. Selection criteria: Does this finding reveal how the system works? Does it advance the structural argument? If not, it stays in the database.

5. **Character ceiling.** Narrative nonfiction can sustain approximately 7-12 named characters before readers lose track. For a network with 80+ investigated targets, this means tiered treatment: 3-5 principals who appear throughout, 8-12 supporting characters introduced in specific sections, and everyone else referenced but not characterized.

6. **Documents as plot points.** Keefe's technique of paraphrasing context, then quoting the devastating line. Caro's technique of building the stakes, then letting the document speak. Ratliff's technique of embedding documents as narrative events ("a key email arrives"). EFTA references and DS10 records should be woven into Ithildin articles using these methods — never dumped as exhibit lists.

7. **Scope management through framing.** Every writer explicitly defines their frame and stays inside it. Keefe keeps *Empire of Pain* focused on the Sacklers even when the opioid crisis is the larger story. McPhee defines his structural diagram before writing. Ithildin articles need explicit scope declarations: "This article follows the money from X to Y through Z. It does not attempt to cover [adjacent thread]."

## 2. Deep Dive: John McPhee

### 2.1 The Structure Essay

McPhee's essay "Structure" in *Draft No. 4* is the most explicit discussion of nonfiction organization by any practitioner. His core beliefs:

- **Structure should be invisible.** "Readers are not supposed to notice the structure; it is meant to be about as visible as someone's bones." The reader experiences the effect of structure — clarity, momentum, revelation — without identifying the mechanism.

- **Structure arises from material, not from templates.** McPhee insists structures should not be imposed upon material but should emerge from within it. The writer's job is to find the structure already latent in the subject.

- **The lead dictates form.** "Settle on a strong lead and allow that opening to dictate the best possible form." The opening scene constrains and enables everything that follows. This is directly relevant to Ithildin: the choice of opening finding or opening document determines the entire article's architecture.

- **A piece must start somewhere, go somewhere, and sit down when it gets there.** This sounds obvious, but McPhee means it structurally: the ending must be implicit in the beginning, and the journey between them must feel inevitable in retrospect.

### 2.2 Structural Diagrams

McPhee physically maps his pieces before writing. He pins structural diagrams — hand-drawn, often incomprehensible to anyone but him — to the bulletin board above his desk. These include:

- **Horizontal line with loops:** Tangents above and below a central storyline, representing departures and returns. The line is the spine; the loops are explorations.

- **Circle with radiating lines:** A central subject with narrative pathways extending outward. Used for pieces organized around a central figure or theme.

- **The lowercase "e":** Used for "Travels in Georgia" — a piece that opens mid-journey, loops back to the beginning, then proceeds chronologically. The structural shape resembles the letter e.

- **The articulated Y:** Used for "A Roomful of Hovings" — a story that begins with a single thread, then splits into two.

- **ABC/D:** The most famous structural innovation. Used for *Encounters with the Archdruid*. Three subjects (A, B, C) are each profiled through their relationship with a fourth subject (D). McPhee pinned a card reading "ABC/D" above his desk and built a triple profile: environmentalist David Brower as the D figure, with three adversaries (a mineral engineer, a resort developer, a dam builder) as A, B, and C.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** The ABC/D structure maps directly onto network investigation. A central entity (D = the network hub) is illuminated through three different connected figures, each revealing a different facet of the system. An article about Deutsche Bank's Epstein relationship could use this: D = the bank, A = the relationship manager, B = the compliance officer who failed, C = the executive who signed off.

### 2.3 The Dual-Spine Technique: *Annals of the Former World*

McPhee's masterwork uses Interstate 80 — a cross-country highway from New York to San Francisco — as the organizing spine for 4.6 billion years of geological history. The technique operates on two registers simultaneously:

- **The spatial spine:** I-80 gives the reader orientation. You always know where you are geographically. The physical journey provides the container.

- **The temporal spine:** At each stop along the highway, McPhee descends into deep geological time. Road cuts expose ancient strata. The geological content provides the information.

The genius is that neither spine alone would work. A purely chronological geological history (starting 4.6 billion years ago) would have no spatial anchor — the reader would be lost in time. A purely geographical account of a road trip would have no depth. By braiding the two, McPhee creates what one critic called "a single thread of words, a human path cut through ancient rock."

**Relevance to Ithildin:** Financial network investigations need the equivalent dual spine. One spine must be something the reader can hold — a chronological timeline, a geographical trail, a single person's trajectory. The other spine is the systemic analysis — the corporate structures, the money flows, the institutional failures. An article about Leon Black's payments to Epstein could use chronology as the spatial spine (year by year, 2012-2017) while each stop on the timeline descends into the corporate structure through which that year's payment moved. The reader always knows *when* they are while learning *how* the system worked.

### 2.4 Point-by-Point with Flashbacks: *Levels of the Game*

*Levels of the Game* follows a single 1968 U.S. Open semifinal between Arthur Ashe and Clark Graebner, point by point, from the initial serve to the final point. But within each point, McPhee flashes back to the players' backgrounds, training, psychology, and social context (Ashe was Black; Graebner was white; this was 1968).

The structure is: present action (the point) → flashback (the context) → return to present (next point). The match provides forward momentum; the flashbacks provide depth.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** This maps to transaction-by-transaction investigation narrative. Each transaction (the "point") is the present-tense action. Between transactions, the narrative can flash back to explain the entity involved, the relationship that enabled the transfer, or the historical context. A DS10 article could follow the money transfers sequentially while using each transfer as a departure point for explaining the entity or mechanism involved.

### 2.5 Portrait-as-System: *The Control of Nature*

McPhee's three long essays in *The Control of Nature* are ostensibly about infrastructure — the Old River Control Structure managing the Mississippi, the cooling of Icelandic lava, the debris flows of the San Gabriel Mountains. But each essay is really about humanity's relationship with natural forces, and each infrastructure project becomes a character study of the engineers and communities involved.

McPhee makes infrastructure comprehensible through three techniques:

1. **Scale through analogy.** He translates engineering quantities into human terms. The reader understands the Mississippi's power not through cubic feet per second but through the consequences of failure.

2. **Character as guide.** Each essay has a human guide — a geologist, an engineer, a local — who walks the reader through the system. The reader trusts the guide and follows them into complexity.

3. **Stakes before mechanism.** McPhee always establishes what will happen if the system fails before explaining how the system works. This creates urgency that sustains attention through technical exposition.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** Financial mechanisms (trusts, LLCs, DPAs, UCC filings) are infrastructure. They can be made comprehensible using McPhee's approach: establish the stakes (what the structure enabled), introduce a human guide (the lawyer, the compliance officer, the victim), then explain the mechanism.

### 2.6 Compression and Selection

McPhee's most quoted principle: "Writing is selection. Just to start a piece of writing you have to choose one word and only one from more than a million in the language."

His selection criterion is deceptively simple: "If something interests you, it goes in — if not, it stays out. That's a crude way to assess things, but it's all you've got."

His compression technique — "greening," adapted from *Time* magazine's editorial practice — involves removing words "so skillfully that no one would notice that anything has been removed." This operates at every level: words within sentences, sentences within paragraphs, paragraphs within sections, sections within pieces.

His length principle: "Ideally, a piece of writing should grow to whatever length is sustained by its selected material — that much and no more."

McPhee gathers "ten times more" information than he uses. The selection process is not about finding what's good — everything is good, or he wouldn't have gathered it — but about finding what serves the structure.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** An automated article generator must implement a selection function. Given 200+ findings on a target, which ones serve the structural argument? McPhee's answer — interest and structural fit — must be translated into computable criteria: Does this finding reveal a mechanism? Does it connect to another thread? Does it contradict the public narrative? Is it sourced to primary documents? The selection algorithm is as important as the writing algorithm.

## 3. Deep Dive: Patrick Radden Keefe

### 3.1 The Eight-Beat Envelope

Keefe's structural method begins with "eight beats on the back of an envelope." Before deep reporting, he identifies:

1. The opening scene
2. The first major transition
3. Subsequent turning points
4. The climactic moment
5. The ending

This stripped-down outline lets him "see the topography of the story" without getting lost in complexity. The eight beats are not an outline — they are a topographic map. He can see the peaks and valleys before he enters the terrain.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** An article generator needs the equivalent of Keefe's envelope: a pre-writing structural map that identifies the key beats before prose generation begins. For an investigation article, these beats might be: (1) the opening revelation, (2) the historical context, (3) the first structural connection, (4) the documentary evidence, (5) the pattern recognition, (6) the systemic implications, (7) the counterargument or missing evidence, (8) the current status and open questions.

### 3.2 The Character-Web Technique

Keefe's signature structural innovation is the character web — a small cast of characters whose intersecting stories illuminate different facets of a system.

***Empire of Pain*** follows three generations of the Sackler family — Arthur, Mortimer/Raymond, and the third generation — each representing a different phase of the dynasty's strategy:

- **Arthur Sackler** (generation 1): The inventor of pharmaceutical marketing. His story explains how the industry was corrupted at the root.
- **Mortimer and Raymond** (generation 2): The builders of Purdue Pharma. Their story explains how a specific company weaponized Arthur's innovations.
- **The third generation**: The inheritors and defenders. Their story explains how wealth insulates against accountability.

This generational structure is not merely chronological — it is argumentative. Each generation represents a deepening of the problem: from innovation to exploitation to impunity.

***Say Nothing*** uses four characters to tell the history of the Troubles in Northern Ireland:

- **Jean McConville:** The victim — a widowed mother of ten, abducted and murdered. Her story is the human cost.
- **Dolours Price:** The perpetrator — an IRA volunteer. Her story is the psychology of political violence.
- **Brendan Hughes:** The commander — an IRA leader who later broke the code of silence. His story is the moral reckoning.
- **Gerry Adams:** The politician — the Sinn Fein leader who denied involvement. His story is the institutional cover-up.

The narrative moves between these four characters across decades, and the reader experiences the Troubles through their intersecting perspectives. Each character illuminates a different facet: victimhood, perpetration, complicity, denial.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** A network investigation article needs a similar character web. For a piece about Deutsche Bank's Epstein accounts, the web might be: (1) Epstein himself — the client who exploited the system, (2) the relationship manager — the enabler within the institution, (3) the compliance officer — the failure point, (4) a victim — the human cost of the financial infrastructure. Each character illuminates a different aspect of systemic failure.

### 3.3 The Documentary Evidence Technique

Keefe integrates primary source documents — depositions, internal memos, emails, court filings — more effectively than almost any other contemporary writer. His method:

1. **Paraphrase first, then quote the devastating line.** Keefe never opens a section with a long quotation. He builds narrative context through paraphrase, establishing the stakes and the characters' positions. Then he drops in the single most revealing line from the document. The effect is like a prosecutorial closing argument: context, context, context, then the damning fact.

2. **Documents as character revelations.** Keefe uses documents to show what people actually said in private, contrasted with what they said in public. A deposition question reveals what a Sackler knew. An internal memo reveals what Purdue concealed. The gap between public and private statements IS the story.

3. **The missing document as evidence.** In *Empire of Pain*, Keefe reports that Ann Hedonia circulated a memo about OxyContin abuse to "all the Sacklers" — a memo that later went missing from Purdue's files. The absence of the document becomes its own evidence. This technique — noting what should exist but does not — is critical for investigative narrative.

4. **The mysterious source.** Keefe received a thumb drive by mail containing key documents. He narrates this moment as a plot point — the arrival of evidence becomes a narrative event, creating suspense and establishing the stakes of the investigation itself.

5. **Digitizing rare materials.** Keefe digitizes archives (Tufts, court filings) to unlock character understanding. He treats documents not merely as evidence but as "keys to psychological motivation." A document is not just proof that something happened — it reveals why someone did what they did.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** EFTA documents, DS10 records, IRS 990 filings, and court records should be integrated using Keefe's method: paraphrase the context, then quote the key line. "Southern Trust Company showed a balance trajectory from zero to $110 million between 2011 and 2015. A single document captures the scale: [quote from specific EFTA record]." The documentary record should never be presented as an exhibit list. It should be narrated.

### 3.4 The Institutional Autopsy

*Empire of Pain* does not just blame the Sacklers. It systematically dissects every institution that enabled them:

- **The FDA** approved OxyContin based on inadequate evidence and later hired the reviewer who approved it.
- **The medical profession** accepted Purdue's claims about low addiction risk without independent verification.
- **The legal system** allowed Purdue to settle cases with NDAs, buying silence from victims.
- **The philanthropic establishment** accepted Sackler donations and named buildings after the family, conferring respectability.

Keefe manages this institutional complexity by treating each institution as a chapter-length subplot rather than trying to explain all of them simultaneously. The reader encounters one failure at a time, each one compounding the previous ones.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** Financial crime investigations involve the same institutional anatomy. The system that enabled Epstein included banks (Deutsche Bank, JPMorgan), law firms (Kirkland & Ellis, Dechert), regulators (SEC, FINRA), prosecutors (SDNY, Palm Beach), and enablers (philanthropic boards, social networks). Each deserves chapter-level treatment, with the failures compounding across sections.

### 3.5 Tone: Prosecutorial Restraint

Keefe's tone is notably restrained given the outrage of his subject matter. He explicitly rejects "cinematic" journalism and "schlocky" screenwriting-influenced narrative. His method:

- **Reserve judgment for the writing phase.** During reporting, Keefe is "open" and "compassionate." During writing, he becomes "pretty bloodless." This separation prevents the tone from becoming either sycophantic or prosecutorial.

- **Let documents carry the emotional weight.** Instead of telling the reader that the Sacklers were callous, Keefe quotes a Sackler family member asking, during a deposition about addiction deaths, whether this could be wrapped up before lunch. The document does the emotional work.

- **Sensory detail over dramatic flourish.** Influenced by Janet Malcolm, Keefe prioritizes "what things smell like" over dramatic reconstruction. He trusts that the facts themselves are dramatic enough.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** Investigative articles about financial crime should adopt Keefe's prosecutorial restraint. The evidence is damning enough without editorializing. Let the $40 million payment from Leon Black to STC speak for itself. Let the EFTA documents carry the weight. The tone should be: "Here is what the records show." Not: "Here is why you should be outraged."

### 3.6 The Legal Shield Narrative

*Empire of Pain* details the Sackler family's defensive strategy in terms directly relevant to Ithildin's subjects:

- **Aggressive legal teams** that threatened journalists and sued publications
- **Settlements with NDAs** that bought victims' silence
- **Strategic philanthropy** that made institutions dependent on Sackler money and thus reluctant to criticize
- **Reputation management** through naming rights, museum donations, and academic funding
- **Corporate restructuring** to shield family wealth from litigation (the eventual Purdue Pharma bankruptcy)

These are precisely the techniques documented in Ithildin's findings about Epstein's network: aggressive legal defense (Kirkland & Ellis, Dershowitz), settlements with NDAs (the 2008 NPA), strategic philanthropy (the various Epstein foundations), and corporate restructuring (the USVI entities, the trust architecture).

Keefe's treatment demonstrates how to narrate these strategies without turning the article into a legal brief. He tells the story of each defensive maneuver as a narrative event with characters, stakes, and consequences.

## 4. Brief Analysis: Caro, Boo, Wright, Ratliff

### 4.1 Robert Caro: Biography as System Analysis

**Core technique:** Using one person's life to explain how an entire system works. *The Power Broker* is not really about Robert Moses — it is about how political power operates in American cities. *The Years of Lyndon Johnson* is not really about Johnson — it is about how the United States Senate, the presidency, and American democracy actually function.

**Making infrastructure compelling.** Caro's "One Mile" chapter — about one mile of the Cross-Bronx Expressway — is the definitive example of making infrastructure into human drama. His technique:

1. **Establish the community first.** Before introducing the highway, Caro spends pages describing East Tremont: its shops, its schools, its immigrant families, its street life. The reader knows what will be destroyed before the destruction begins.

2. **Introduce a human protagonist.** Lillian Edelstein, a "local housewife," becomes the reader's avatar in the fight against Moses. She is not a politician or an activist — she is someone the reader can identify with.

3. **Contrast scale.** Moses built 627 miles of roads. Caro zooms into one mile. The contrast between the system's vastness and the individual's vulnerability IS the argument.

4. **Show the alternative that wasn't taken.** A straight route would have destroyed only a handful of apartments. The route Moses chose destroyed an entire neighborhood. The existence of the better option makes the destruction feel not just tragic but chosen — an act of power, not necessity.

5. **Sensory detail of destruction.** Vandals stripping properties, blasting through solid rock, tenements crumbling, deafening noise, pervasive dust. Caro does not summarize the destruction — he makes the reader experience it.

**Research method: "Turn every page."** Caro's mentor taught him: "Turn every goddam page." This exhaustive approach — 522 interviews for *The Power Broker* alone, physical relocation to Texas Hill Country for three years — produces the documentary foundation for narrative authority. The reader trusts Caro because the evidence base is visibly comprehensive.

**Concentric circle strategy for access.** When key subjects refuse to cooperate, Caro starts with outer circles of acquaintances, building a comprehensive portrait that eventually draws the inner circle into conversation. People talk because they realize Caro already knows most of the story.

**Thematic chapter structure.** Caro does not follow strict chronology. He devotes entire chapters to single projects or themes — one chapter on parks, another on highways, another on housing — while excluding reference to other events happening at the same time. Each chapter becomes a self-contained dramatic mini-narrative within the book's larger arc. This technique is directly relevant to Ithildin's thread-based organization: each article can follow one investigative thread to its conclusion without trying to interleave all threads simultaneously.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** Caro's approach validates the platform's instinct to organize by investigative thread rather than by chronology. Thread 3 (Deutsche Bank Pipeline) should be its own article series, told as a self-contained system narrative, just as Caro devotes entire chapters to single infrastructure projects. The "One Mile" technique — zooming from the systemic to the specific, from 627 miles to one neighborhood — maps to zooming from a $67 million account roster to a single suspicious transaction.

### 4.2 Katherine Boo: The Invisible Narrator

**Core technique:** Narrative nonfiction built entirely from documented evidence, with the author invisible. *Behind the Beautiful Forevers* reads like a novel, but every scene, every quote, every detail is documented through Boo's three years of immersive reporting in Annawadi, a Mumbai slum.

**The evidence standard.** Boo spent 2007-2011 gathering photographs, audio and video recordings, interviews, and official records. She does not appear in her own narrative — "an interview-based narrative in which the interviewer never appears." The author's note at the end outlines her research methodology and verification process.

**Making truth surpass fiction.** Critics describe Boo as "one of those rare, deep-digging journalists who can make truth surpass fiction." Her technique: treat real people as characters with full interiority, based entirely on documented observation and interview. The narrative reads as omniscient, but every observation has an evidentiary basis.

**The philosophy of invisibility.** Boo states: "When I settle into a place, listening and watching, I don't try to fool myself that the stories of the individuals are themselves arguments. I just believe that better arguments, maybe even better policies, get formulated when we know more about ordinary lives."

**Relevance to Ithildin:** Boo's invisible-narrator technique is the right model for automated investigative articles. The platform should not editorialize or speculate — it should present the documented evidence with narrative skill but no visible authorial presence. "The records show" rather than "We believe." The evidence speaks; the platform organizes.

### 4.3 Lawrence Wright: Parallel Tracks Converging

**Core technique:** Multiple parallel storylines that the reader knows will converge, creating narrative suspense from historical inevitability.

*The Looming Tower* follows four figures on parallel tracks:

- **Osama bin Laden and Ayman al-Zawahiri** on the terrorist track
- **John O'Neill** (FBI counterterrorism chief) on the American track
- **Prince Turki al-Faisal** (Saudi intelligence) on the geopolitical track

The reader knows these tracks will converge on September 11, 2001. The suspense comes not from whether they will converge but from *how* — specifically, from the institutional failures (FBI-CIA rivalry, Saudi stonewalling) that prevented the convergence from happening in time.

Wright's dual-track technique works because:

1. **Each track has its own narrative logic.** The al-Qaeda track follows the internal politics of jihadism. The FBI track follows bureaucratic infighting. Each is comprehensible on its own terms.

2. **The tracks illuminate each other.** When the reader sees CIA withholding intelligence from FBI in one chapter, then sees al-Qaeda operatives entering the United States in the next, the juxtaposition creates the argument: institutional failure enabled catastrophe.

3. **Small cast per track.** Wright does not try to follow dozens of people. Each track has 2-3 central characters. The total cast across all tracks stays manageable.

**Research foundation:** Five years, hundreds of interviews, conducted across ten countries. Like Caro, Wright's authority derives from exhaustive research that is visible in the text's specificity.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** The converging-tracks structure suits investigation articles about institutional failure. Thread 3 (Deutsche Bank) and Thread 7 (Kirkland & Ellis) are parallel tracks that converge in Epstein's legal and financial operations. An article could follow the banking track and the legal track separately, showing how each institution's internal logic led to the same failure point.

### 4.4 Evan Ratliff: Criminal Network as Serial Narrative

**Core technique:** Explaining the structure of a criminal enterprise while maintaining narrative momentum, using serialized publication to match the progressive revelation of the investigation itself.

*The Mastermind* follows Paul Le Roux — a programmer who built an online prescription drug empire that evolved into a global criminal conglomerate spanning drug trafficking, arms dealing, gold smuggling, and contract murder.

**Structural innovations:**

1. **Serial publication.** Originally published as a seven-part serial on The Atavist, the story's structure mirrors the investigation itself: each installment reveals new layers of Le Roux's operation, just as Ratliff discovered them.

2. **The unreliable narrator problem.** Ratliff describes operating in "a milieu lousy with unreliable narrators" — criminals, informants, intelligence operatives — and his technique of "triangulating where possible to separate fact from legend." This is directly relevant to OSINT investigations where sources have competing interests.

3. **The flip.** Le Roux's arrest and cooperation with the DEA creates a structural pivot: the criminal becomes the informant, and the narrative shifts from pursuit to dismantlement. The story turns again when Le Roux begins bringing down his own organization.

4. **Documents as plot points.** Court filings, communications intercepts, and financial records are embedded in the narrative as events — they arrive, they reveal, they change the direction of the story.

**Atmospheric specificity.** Ratliff's prose is described as "crisp" and "atmospheric" — he does not sacrifice scene-setting for exposition. The reader knows what Manila smells like, what a DEA operation feels like, what a courtroom sounds like.

**Relevance to Ithildin:** Ratliff's serial structure is a model for progressive disclosure in investigative articles. Rather than dumping all findings at once, an article can reveal the network's structure layer by layer: first the visible entities, then the hidden connections, then the money flows, then the systemic implications. Each layer is a narrative beat, and each beat ends with a revelation that reframes what came before.

## 5. Cross-Cutting Analysis

### 5.1 Structure as Argument

All six writers use structure to make arguments, not merely to organize information.

| Writer | Structure | Argument Made by Structure |
|--------|-----------|---------------------------|
| McPhee (ABC/D) | Three profiles connected through a fourth figure | Complex problems cannot be understood from a single perspective |
| McPhee (dual spine) | Spatial journey + temporal depth | Deep time is comprehensible only through present experience |
| Keefe (character web) | Intersecting lives across generations | Systemic failure compounds across time; individual blame is insufficient |
| Keefe (institutional autopsy) | Sequential chapter-length dissections | Every enabling institution shares responsibility |
| Caro (thematic chapters) | Single-project deep dives | Power is exercised through specific mechanisms, not abstract authority |
| Wright (parallel tracks) | Converging storylines | Institutional failure is the product of parallel, independent bad decisions |
| Ratliff (serial revelation) | Progressive disclosure | Criminal networks are understood layer by layer, not all at once |
| Boo (invisible narrator) | Evidence-only narration | Reality is more devastating than any editorial could convey |

**Recommendation for Ithildin:** Each article type should have a structural template that embodies an argument:

- **Dossier articles** (about a person): Use McPhee's ABC/D or Caro's thematic-chapter structure. Argument: the person is a node in a system, not an isolated actor.
- **Thread articles** (about an investigation thread): Use Wright's parallel-tracks structure. Argument: multiple institutions failed in complementary ways.
- **Financial trace articles** (about money flows): Use McPhee's dual-spine or *Levels of the Game* point-by-point structure. Argument: each transaction reveals a mechanism.
- **Network articles** (about how entities connect): Use Keefe's character-web structure. Argument: the network is greater than any individual.

### 5.2 The "Too Many People" Problem

Investigative narratives about networks share the Russian-novel problem: the reader cannot track all the characters. Each writer's solution:

- **McPhee:** Strict limitation. Rarely more than 3-5 characters per piece. Deep profiles of the few rather than shallow mentions of the many. In ABC/D structure, only 4 named characters carry the narrative.

- **Keefe:** Recurring cast with re-introduction. *Empire of Pain* has perhaps 20-25 named characters, but only 8-10 appear in multiple chapters. Each time a character reappears after an absence, Keefe re-identifies them with a brief tag ("Kathe Sackler, Richard's daughter" or "the former sales rep who had raised concerns").

- **Caro:** One protagonist, everyone else defined relationally. In *The Power Broker*, there are hundreds of named people, but every single one is defined by their relationship to Moses: ally, opponent, victim, tool. The reader tracks Moses and understands everyone else through their position relative to him.

- **Wright:** Small cast per track. *The Looming Tower* has perhaps 15-20 significant characters, but no more than 4-5 appear in any single chapter. The parallel-track structure creates natural compartments.

- **Boo:** Focus on 3-4 families. *Behind the Beautiful Forevers* follows a handful of families in Annawadi, using their intersecting lives to represent larger dynamics. Named characters total perhaps 15, but the core cast is 5-6.

- **Ratliff:** Centered on one figure. Le Roux is the gravitational center; everyone else is introduced, defined by their role in his operation, and then followed only as long as they intersect with the main narrative.

**What the cognitive research suggests:** Working memory can hold approximately 4-7 discrete items (Miller's "magic number"). Narrative can stretch this — through re-introduction, relational definition, and structural compartmentalization — but probably not beyond 12-15 tracked characters in a single reading experience. Fiction writing guides converge on a "rule of seven" for major characters, with the understanding that minor characters can exceed this if they are clearly tagged and compartmentalized.

**Recommendation for Ithildin:** A tiered character treatment:

- **Tier 1 (3-5 principals):** Appear throughout the article. Fully characterized. The reader tracks their names, roles, and arc.
- **Tier 2 (8-12 supporting):** Appear in specific sections. Re-introduced with a role tag each time they appear ("Darren Indyke, Epstein's personal attorney" — never just "Indyke" after an absence).
- **Tier 3 (unlimited references):** Mentioned by name but not characterized. Treated as evidence ("records show payments to [name]") rather than as narrative characters.

For the 80+ investigated targets in Ithildin, this means a single article cannot profile more than about 5 people. Network-level articles must choose their 3-5 representative characters carefully, using McPhee's ABC/D logic: each character should illuminate a different facet of the system.

### 5.3 Documentary Evidence in Narrative

The core challenge: how do you integrate primary sources (EFTA documents, court filings, financial records, emails) into narrative without killing the pacing?

**Technique 1: Keefe's "Paraphrase, then quote."**

The method: Build narrative context through paraphrase. Establish what was happening, who was involved, what was at stake. Then drop in the single most devastating line from the document. The quote lands with force because the reader has the context to understand why it matters.

Example structure: "In 2004, Ann Hedonia, a Purdue employee, conducted research into OxyContin abuse patterns. She compiled her findings and circulated a memo to, as she described it in a later deposition, 'all the Sacklers' involved in running the company. That memo later went missing from Purdue's files."

Note: the deposition quote ("all the Sacklers") is only four words. But it is the four words that matter. The surrounding paraphrase does the work of establishing context, and the brief quote delivers the revelation.

**Technique 2: Caro's "Build stakes, then let the document speak."**

The method: Construct the dramatic situation — the community facing destruction, the political deal being made — and then, at the moment of maximum tension, introduce the document. The document functions as the climactic evidence that confirms what the narrative has been building toward.

Caro builds pages of narrative about East Tremont, about Lillian Edelstein's organizing efforts, about the community's hope — and then introduces the 90-day eviction notice. The document is the dramatic resolution.

**Technique 3: Ratliff's "Document as plot point."**

The method: Treat the arrival or discovery of a document as a narrative event. "A key email arrives." "A thumb drive appears in the mail." "Court filings unsealed in 2019 reveal..." The document is not cited as evidence — it happens.

This technique works especially well for investigation narratives where the researcher's own process of discovery is part of the story.

**Technique 4: The missing document.**

Both Keefe and Caro use the absence of documents as evidence. The memo that went missing. The records that were destroyed. The filing that should exist but does not. In investigation narrative, what's missing is often more significant than what's present.

**Recommendation for Ithildin:** Use all four techniques, matched to context:

- For individual findings with a key quote: Keefe's paraphrase-then-quote
- For building toward a major revelation: Caro's stakes-then-document
- For the investigation's own discoveries: Ratliff's document-as-event
- For suspicious gaps: The missing-document technique

EFTA references should never appear as bare citations ("EFTA02336502 shows..."). They should be narrativized: "A document from the government's cache of seized records — filed as EFTA02336502 — captures the arrangement in a single line: [quote]."

### 5.4 Chronological vs. Thematic Structure

The perennial structural question: should an investigative piece follow time or follow topic?

**Chronological strengths:**
- Shows how situations developed
- Creates narrative momentum (what happened next?)
- Familiar to readers — no structural learning curve
- Good for showing escalation and compounding failures

**Chronological weaknesses:**
- Obscures connections between events separated in time
- Forces the reader to hold pattern recognition across long temporal spans
- Interleaves unrelated threads if multiple things were happening simultaneously
- Buries structural insights inside sequential event reporting

**Thematic strengths:**
- Shows patterns across time
- Groups related evidence regardless of when it occurred
- Enables structural arguments ("here are four instances of the same technique")
- Natural for network analysis — follow the connection, not the clock

**Thematic weaknesses:**
- Loses temporal causation (the reader does not see how A led to B)
- Can feel static — pattern identification without forward momentum
- Requires the reader to trust the writer's categorization
- Harder to create suspense without temporal progression

**McPhee's hybrid:** Thematic spine with chronological episodes. The overall structure follows themes, but within each thematic section, events are presented chronologically. This captures both pattern and causation.

McPhee notes that "for the story of a journey," chronological alternatives "can be unpromising and confusing." But for the story of a system, pure chronology obscures the system's logic.

**Recommendation for Ithildin content types:**

| Content Type | Recommended Structure | Rationale |
|-------------|----------------------|-----------|
| **Person dossier** | Thematic with chronological sub-sections | The person's role in the system is the argument; timeline within each role |
| **Financial trace** | Chronological spine with thematic departures | Money flows are inherently sequential; departures explain mechanisms |
| **Thread narrative** | McPhee hybrid — thematic spine, chronological episodes | The thread's systemic significance is the argument; each episode is a case study |
| **Network overview** | Thematic (by cluster/function) | Network structure is spatial, not temporal |
| **Event investigation** | Chronological with institutional interludes | Specific events unfold in time; institutional context requires thematic treatment |
| **Systemic analysis** | Fully thematic | Patterns across time and entities are the entire point |

### 5.5 Compression and the Evidence Budget

Every writer in this cluster has vastly more material than they use. Their selection frameworks:

**McPhee:** "If something interests you, it goes in — if not, it stays out." Crude but honest. McPhee trusts his own editorial instinct, refined over decades. He gathers ten times more than he needs and lets the structure determine what survives. The piece grows to "whatever length is sustained by its selected material — that much and no more."

**Keefe:** Entire storylines were excluded from *Empire of Pain*. The selection criterion appears to be: does this advance the structural argument about how the Sackler dynasty operated? If a finding is interesting but tangential to the family-as-system thesis, it does not survive. Keefe's eight-beat envelope forces early selection — if a finding does not fit into one of the eight beats, it is a candidate for exclusion.

**Caro:** Famous for cutting. His first draft of *The Power Broker* was reportedly much longer than the published 1,246 pages. His editor Robert Gottlieb cut hundreds of pages while Caro simultaneously added new material. The selection criterion: does this scene show how power works? If it shows something about Moses's personality but not about the mechanics of power, it is expendable.

**Boo:** Three years of observation compressed into approximately 250 pages covering roughly four years of narrative time. Her selection criterion appears to be representativeness: does this scene illuminate a dynamic that is broadly true of Annawadi, not just true of this particular moment?

**Recommendation for Ithildin:** A finding-selection framework for articles:

1. **Structural fit.** Does this finding serve one of the article's 8 structural beats?
2. **Mechanism revelation.** Does this finding show HOW the system works, not just THAT it exists?
3. **Documentary strength.** Is this finding supported by primary sources (EFTA, court filings, 990s), or is it inference/synthesis?
4. **Uniqueness.** Does this finding add information that no other included finding provides? (Three sources showing the same fact = redundancy, not emphasis.)
5. **Narrative power.** Does this finding have a quotable detail, a surprising juxtaposition, or a human moment?

A target with 200 findings might yield an article using 30-40 findings. The other 160 remain in the database, available for other articles, cross-references, and graph analysis.

### 5.6 Scope Management

How do you keep a piece focused when the network connects to everything?

**Keefe's answer:** Define the family. *Empire of Pain* is about the Sacklers, not about the opioid crisis. The opioid crisis is the context, but the family is the frame. Everything in the book is either about the Sacklers or about how institutions responded to the Sacklers. The broader crisis enters only insofar as it illuminates the family's responsibility.

**McPhee's answer:** Define the diagram. The structural diagram drawn before writing determines what is inside the piece and what is outside. If a fascinating tangent does not fit the diagram, it does not go in. The diagram is the frame.

**Caro's answer:** Define the protagonist. *The Power Broker* is about Robert Moses. Everything in the book — every institution, every community, every political deal — is there because it intersects with Moses. If a significant event in New York City history did not involve Moses, it is not in the book.

**Wright's answer:** Define the convergence point. *The Looming Tower* is about the road to 9/11. Every parallel track exists because it converges on that event. Tracks that do not converge are not followed.

**Recommendation for Ithildin:** Every article needs an explicit scope declaration, defined before writing begins:

- **For dossier articles:** "This article follows [person] through [system]. It does not attempt to cover [adjacent thread]."
- **For thread articles:** "This article traces [thread] from [origin] to [current state]. Adjacent threads are referenced where they intersect but not independently developed."
- **For network articles:** "This article maps [cluster/function]. Entities outside this cluster appear only where they connect to it."

The scope declaration is the structural equivalent of McPhee's diagram: it defines what is inside and what is outside before any prose is generated.

## 6. Specific Questions Answered

### What's McPhee's structural diagram for a piece about a network?

McPhee has not written a network-investigation piece per se, but his ABC/D structure is the closest analog. The D figure (the central connector) is profiled through their relationships with A, B, and C (peripheral figures who each reveal a different facet). For a network, the generalization would be: choose one hub node as D, then select 3-4 peripheral nodes that represent different functional roles in the network (financial, legal, social, operational). Each peripheral profile illuminates both the hub and the network's structure.

An alternative McPhee structure for network writing: the dual-spine model from *Annals of the Former World*. One spine is the network map itself (spatial); the other is the investigative narrative (temporal). The reader moves across the network geographically while the writer descends into documentary depth at each node.

### How does Keefe integrate 30+ deposition citations into a single chapter?

Through radical selectivity and the paraphrase-then-quote technique. Keefe does not cite 30 depositions in a chapter — he cites perhaps 6-8, selecting the single most revealing line from each. The rest of the evidentiary base is compressed into narrative paraphrase. The reader experiences a chapter that feels richly documented because every quoted line is devastating, not because every document is quoted.

His method: (1) read all 30 depositions, (2) extract the single most revealing line from each, (3) select the 6-8 that advance the chapter's argument, (4) build narrative context around each selected quote, (5) present the quotes in an order that creates cumulative force.

### Maximum named characters before readers lose track?

No single definitive number exists in the research, but converging evidence suggests:

- **Working memory:** 4-7 items (Miller's number)
- **Fiction writing consensus:** 7 major characters maximum, with unlimited minor characters if clearly tagged
- **Narrative nonfiction practice:** McPhee uses 3-5; Keefe uses 8-10 recurring characters with perhaps 20 total named; Caro uses 1 protagonist with hundreds of named people defined relationally; Wright uses 4-5 principals with 15-20 total named
- **The Russian-novel problem:** Readers of *War and Peace* and *Anna Karenina* famously struggle with character tracking. Tolstoy mitigates this with patronymics and family groupings, but readers still complain.

**Practical ceiling:** 12-15 named characters who require individual tracking. Beyond that, characters should be identified by role ("the relationship manager," "the compliance officer") rather than by name, or grouped into collective entities ("the Sackler family," "the inner circle").

### How does Caro make infrastructure compelling?

Five specific techniques, enumerated above in section 4.1:

1. Establish the community first (what will be destroyed)
2. Introduce a human protagonist (whom the reader identifies with)
3. Contrast scale (627 miles vs. one mile)
4. Show the alternative that was not taken (the better route that existed)
5. Sensory detail of destruction (noise, dust, fear)

The underlying principle: infrastructure is compelling when the reader understands its human consequences. The mechanism is interesting only after the stakes are established.

### Best existing longform narrative about a financial crime network?

Several candidates, each with different strengths:

- **Tom Burgis, *Kleptopia* (2020):** Four interwoven narratives about global kleptocracy, structured like a Greek drama (Crisis-Chrysalis-Metamorphosis). Strengths: scope, atmospheric prose, structural ambition. Weaknesses: some critics found the four-story structure diffuse. Most directly relevant to Ithildin's multi-thread challenge.

- **Tom Wright and Bradley Hope, *Billion Dollar Whale* (2018):** The 1MDB scandal, centered on Jho Low. Strengths: single-protagonist clarity, financial mechanism explanation, narrative momentum. Weaknesses: simplifies institutional context for readability. Best model for a single-target financial crime narrative.

- **Dan McCrum, *Money Men* (2022):** The Wirecard fraud, told from the investigating journalist's perspective. Strengths: first-person investigation narrative, progressive revelation, financial detail. Weaknesses: limited to the journalist's viewpoint.

- **Jake Bernstein, *Secrecy World* (2017):** The Panama Papers investigation. Strengths: explains offshore finance structures, integrates massive documentary evidence. Weaknesses: structural complexity sometimes overwhelms narrative momentum.

- **Keefe, *Empire of Pain* (2021):** Not strictly about financial crime but about how wealth, legal strategy, and institutional corruption interact. Structurally the most sophisticated model.

For Ithildin's purposes, *Kleptopia* is the most relevant structural model because it faces the same challenge: multiple parallel narratives about interconnected financial crimes that must converge into a coherent systemic argument. Its weaknesses (diffuse structure, critic complaints about tracking four stories simultaneously) are precisely the problems Ithildin must solve.

## 7. Recommendations for Ithildin Article Structure

### 7.1 Article Type Templates

**Template A: The Dossier (Person-as-System)**
- Structure: McPhee's portrait-as-system + Caro's thematic chapters
- Opening: A single revealing document or moment
- Body: 3-5 thematic sections (financial role, legal shield, network position, institutional enabling, current status)
- Each section: chronological internally, thematic overall
- Character budget: 1 principal + 5-8 supporting, defined relationally
- Evidence integration: Keefe's paraphrase-then-quote throughout
- Closing: What this person's role reveals about the system
- Length: 3,000-5,000 words
- Findings budget: 25-40 from the target's 200+

**Template B: The Thread Narrative (System Autopsy)**
- Structure: Wright's parallel tracks + Keefe's institutional autopsy
- Opening: The systemic failure in a single scene
- Body: 3-4 institutional tracks, each followed to its failure point
- Convergence: A section showing how the failures compounded
- Character budget: 3-4 principals (one per track) + 8-10 supporting
- Evidence integration: Caro's build-stakes-then-document for major revelations
- Closing: What the system enabled and whether it has been reformed
- Length: 5,000-8,000 words
- Findings budget: 40-80 across all tracks

**Template C: The Financial Trace (Follow the Money)**
- Structure: McPhee's *Levels of the Game* point-by-point + dual spine
- Opening: The total — the number that captures the scale
- Spine: Transaction chronology (sequential money movements)
- Departures: At each transaction, explain the entity or mechanism involved
- Character budget: 2-3 principals (sender, receiver, intermediary) + 5-8 entities
- Evidence integration: Ratliff's document-as-event (each filing is a plot point)
- Closing: Where the money ended up and what it bought
- Length: 2,500-4,000 words
- Findings budget: 15-30 financial findings

**Template D: The Network Map (Connections Article)**
- Structure: McPhee's ABC/D + Keefe's character web
- Opening: The connection the public does not know about
- Body: 3-5 cluster profiles, each centered on a hub node
- Connections: Explicit sections showing how clusters link
- Character budget: 4-5 hub nodes + unlimited references by role
- Evidence integration: Mixed — quotes for key documents, references for supporting evidence
- Closing: What the network's structure reveals about its function
- Length: 4,000-6,000 words
- Findings budget: 30-50 connection-oriented findings

### 7.2 The Pre-Writing Protocol

Before any article generation, the system should produce:

1. **Scope declaration:** One sentence defining what is inside and outside the article
2. **Structural template selection:** Which template (A/B/C/D) fits this content
3. **Beat map:** Keefe's 8 beats on the envelope — the opening, transitions, climax, and close
4. **Character budget:** Who are the Tier 1 principals? Who are Tier 2 supporting characters? Everyone else is Tier 3 (referenced by role).
5. **Findings selection:** Which of the target's findings survive the evidence-budget criteria (structural fit, mechanism revelation, documentary strength, uniqueness, narrative power)?
6. **Missing-evidence inventory:** What should exist but does not? What gaps are investigatively significant?

This protocol mirrors McPhee's structural diagram, Keefe's envelope, and Caro's outline — adapted for automated production.

### 7.3 Structural Rules

1. **Never open with biography.** Open with a document, a transaction, a moment, or a connection. The person's biography is context, not structure. (McPhee: the lead dictates form.)

2. **Re-introduce every character after absence.** If a name has not appeared for more than 500 words, re-identify them with a role tag. (Keefe's recurring-cast technique.)

3. **One system per section.** Do not interleave financial analysis with legal strategy with social network analysis. Each section addresses one system. (Caro's thematic-chapter approach.)

4. **Establish stakes before mechanism.** Before explaining how a trust structure works, establish what it enabled — what money it hid, what liability it shielded. (McPhee's *Control of Nature* technique.)

5. **Quote sparingly; paraphrase liberally.** The ratio should be approximately 1 line of direct quote for every 5-8 lines of paraphrase. Each quote must be the most revealing line available. (Keefe's technique.)

6. **Use the missing-document technique.** When records should exist but do not, say so. The absence is evidence. (Keefe + Caro.)

7. **End with open questions, not conclusions.** Investigative articles should close with what remains unknown, what leads are open, what the system might still be doing. (This distinguishes investigation from prosecution — the platform is gathering evidence, not issuing verdicts.)
