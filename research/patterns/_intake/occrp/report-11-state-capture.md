# OCCRP Evidence Ontology — Regional State Capture and Elite Networks

Compiled 2026-07-29. Web-researched from the six project hubs specified in the sampling frame and the canonical story pages exposed by those hubs. This report analyzes 11 stories across all six seed projects. It does not treat project-page hosting as proof of editorial leadership, and it does not treat the same document republished by several outlets as corroboration.

## Scope, attribution, and dependency coding

The six seed hubs are [Uncensored: The Kyrgyzstan Project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/uncensored-the-kyrgyzstan-project), [The Matraimov Kingdom](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom), [The State Capture Papers](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers), [Plunder and Patronage in the Heart of Central Asia](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia), [Corruptistan: Azerbaijan](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan), and [Unholy Alliances](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances). Stories were selected only after fetching those hubs.

Attribution is recorded at article level. The Matraimov landing page expressly calls the project a collaboration among **OCCRP, Kloop, RFE/RL’s Radio Azattyk, and Bellingcat**; the Plunder landing page describes a joint **RFE/RL–OCCRP–Kloop** team and calls Kloop OCCRP’s Kyrgyz member center; the Unholy Alliances credits name **MANS** in Montenegro and **CINS** in Serbia alongside OCCRP journalists ([Matraimov credits](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom), [Plunder credits](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia), [Unholy Alliances credits](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances)). For the Panama Papers story, **Süddeutsche Zeitung obtained the leak and ICIJ organized the collaboration; OCCRP published and reported its version with RFE/RL contributions** ([OCCRP Panama Papers page](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers), [mining story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)). None of the selected pages attributes coordination to Forbidden Stories, so no such role is inferred.

Input-dependency classes answer what a new outside investigator, without privileged consortium access, could reproduce:

- **(a) open-record-runnable**: decisive sources can be independently obtained today, including public filings, registries, official publications, and open web evidence.
- **(b) re-anchoring**: an insider or leak supplied the discovery lead, but a material verification layer is reproducible in public records. The public and non-public halves are separated explicitly.
- **(c) leak-dependent**: the exact central proposition still rests materially on private records. A public substitute can generate a lead or partial reconstruction but not the same finding.
- **(d) closed-platform-dependent**: the work requires Aleph Pro, a consortium document room, or another member-gated search system. None of the 11 selected stories falls here.

### All the President’s Men: State Projects Handed to Apparent Proxies in Kyrgyzstan (2024) — Secrecy reforms hid $137 million in state projects behind five interlinked, politically connected contractors

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan
- **Partner/awards**: OCCRP-hosted joint reporting by Eldiyar Arykbaev (OCCRP), Bolot Temirov (Temirov Live), and Kloop; partner editions appeared at Kloop, Kazakhstan’s Vlast, and Temirov LIVE. The story calls Temirov Live an OCCRP member center and describes Kloop as a collaborating outlet. No ICIJ or Forbidden Stories coordination is stated ([article credits and partner editions](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
- **What they found**:
  - Reporters identified **at least 11 projects** run through the Presidential Administrative Directorate. Costs were available for only six, totaling **$137 million**; the other five had disappeared behind reduced procurement and budget transparency ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
  - Five apparent contractors were interlinked through directors, shareholders, workers, telephone labels, hometown and employment ties. Their formal owners or directors included friends and associates of President Sadyr Japarov, his son, or Directorate chief Kanybek Tumanbayev ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
  - Evro Shpal was incorporated immediately before a state railway-tie project, initially under an obscure owner and later transferred to people linked to Tumanbayev. The Economy Ministry confirmed the private company owned the factory; a railway insider said state money and material had built it before it passed into private hands ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
  - A published decree disclosed that Inshaat Stroy Building, the previously hidden Presidential Palace contractor, would receive three Bishkek land plots that experts valued at up to **$77 million** ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **capture-by-opacity** — legal or administrative transparency is removed before discretionary public spending expands; **proxy-contractor graph** — nominally independent vendors resolve to one patronage circle through people, addresses, employment, and shared labor; **state-to-private asset drift** — a publicly financed facility or land benefit emerges in private hands without a visible selection or transfer process.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Corporate registry records** — incorporation, shareholder, director, and re-registration histories collected after procurement records became unavailable; used to connect the five companies and time their creation or transfer ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
  - **Land records and official decrees** — cadastral ownership and the decree granting Presidential Palace land; obtained from official/public records and direct agency requests ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
  - **Government video and state-media captions** — a worker identified as belonging to Sapat Zhol and contractor-linked people present at project sites; obtained from public broadcasts ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
  - **Social media and open-web evidence** — TikTok factory videos, worker overlap, photographs, GetContact labels, and posts by associates; collected from the public web, with the source accounts listed by OCCRP ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
  - **Confidential operational sources** — a Directorate insider, a railway insider, and company insiders supplied contractor names and alleged true-control details; reporter-obtained, not publicly reproducible ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(b) re-anchoring.** The non-public half was discovery of hidden contractor names and assertions that Tumanbayev issued orders to nominal owners. The reusable public half is substantial: enumerate projects from official announcements; extract companies from decrees, state-TV captions, and worker posts; join those companies to incorporation and land histories; connect owners/directors to public appointments, prior companies, photographs, and shared workforces. That public half can reproduce the contractor network and opacity finding, but not prove ultimate control or state financing of Evro Shpal without insiders.
- **Detection signature**: **hidden-contract proxy join** — state project announcements and official site footage joined to corporate and land records on project site, contractor name, director/shareholder, incorporation date, and parcel, then joined to appointments and social connections on person/phone/employer, revealed five nominal contractors converging on one presidential-administration circle.
- **Corroboration structure**: Insider names were treated as leads; ministry confirmation, corporate re-registration, state-broadcast captions, social-media presence at sites, worker overlap, and land decrees provided independent anchors. The article distinguishes documentary links from the stronger insider allegation of beneficial control ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
- **Methodology notes**: OCCRP states the collection path in the article: reporters assembled incorporation and land records, analyzed social posts, made direct government requests, and used multiple insiders; it also lists the visual-source accounts and says its Fact-Checking Desk reviewed the work ([method disclosure](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)). **[Inferred]** The core data model is project → site → observed contractor → legal entity → owners/directors → patronage ties, with temporal edges for incorporation and public appointment.
- **Official impact**: No discrete official investigation or policy response attributable to this May 2024 story was identified on its page; Japarov, Tumanbayev, the Directorate, airport management, and named owners did not answer detailed questions ([responses section](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/all-the-presidents-men-state-projects-handed-to-apparent-proxies-in-kyrgyzstan)).
- **Generalization**: The pattern appears wherever governments exempt state enterprises or emergency bodies from tender rules. A generic detector watches procurement-law exemptions and disappearing budget portals, inventories announced projects, resolves visible contractors from permits/site media, and flags newly formed or frequently transferred vendors whose principals overlap executive-office staff, relatives, campaign workers, or prior family businesses.

### How to Build a Customs Empire (2020) — Three Kyrgyz customs terminals became chokepoints controlled through relatives and inexperienced proxies

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire
- **Partner/awards**: Explicit project collaboration among OCCRP, **Kloop**, RFE/RL’s **Radio Azattyk**, and **Bellingcat**. The landing page lists the four organizations and protects unnamed local reporters for safety; no ICIJ or Forbidden Stories lead is stated ([project credits](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom)).
- **What they found**:
  - The Matraimov and Abdukadyr families appeared to control **three key customs terminals**, which had become the only practical option for trucks importing Chinese goods into Kyrgyzstan; one southern terminal was used almost exclusively by the Abdukadyrs’ Abu Sahiy network ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire)).
  - UniLab, a licensed **37-hectare** logistics center, opened in 2016 but received no trucks while customs officers redirected traffic elsewhere. It began receiving the northern-route traffic only after the founder and his partners sold it in November 2016 and ownership cycled through a wrestler, a police officer, and people connected to the Abdukadyr network ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire)).
  - Mega Logistik’s formal founder was 21, while land beneath its terminal was partly owned by men tied to the Matraimov family; the facility processed almost exclusively Abu Sahiy trucks at the Uzbek border ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire)).
  - Several nominal owners had no relevant operating history. One wrestler told reporters, “I don’t work”; another temporary shareholder was a senior police officer who later became a mayor and parliamentary candidate in a Matraimov-associated party ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **regulatory-chokepoint capture** — a network gains control of the mandatory physical/administrative gate through which rivals must pass; **proxy succession** — a valuable licensed company cycles rapidly through implausible owners before reaching the beneficiary network; **market-access coercion** — officials redirect customers away from an independent competitor until ownership changes.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Company histories** — registry records for UniLab, Fast Cargo, Tarim Trans, Mega Logistik, and related firms; obtained from Kyrgyz corporate records and used to reconstruct short ownership tenures ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire)).
  - **Land and license records** — parcel owners beneath terminals plus warehouse and customs-service permissions; public/requestable administrative records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire)).
  - **Social media and local-language reporting** — photographs linked nominal owners to network figures; Radio Azattyk documented UniLab’s difficulties; obtained from public profiles and partner archives ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire)).
  - **Industry and operational interviews** — drivers, logistics professionals, former partners, and three protected sources described truck redirection, terminal use, and coercive pressure; reporter-obtained ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire)).
  - **Prior source archive** — the earlier Saimaiti material seeded the Abdukadyr–Matraimov network and customs-fraud hypothesis; obtained from an insider and later published by OCCRP ([Saimaiti archive](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/saimaitis-archive)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(b) re-anchoring.** The prior insider archive and interviews supplied the corrupt-network hypothesis and explanations for why traffic shifted. The reusable public half is the terminal map, license status, land ownership, corporate succession, political appointments, and visible social graph. Those records can independently flag captured chokepoints and implausible proxies, but they cannot alone prove official coercion, bribe collection, or the reason customs officers redirected trucks.
- **Detection signature**: **chokepoint ownership succession** — customs licenses and truck-route destinations compared before and after ownership changes on terminal, date, and traffic corridor, then joined to corporate/land records on company, shareholder, director, and parcel, revealed that traffic migrated to facilities after control passed to Matraimov–Abdukadyr associates and proxies.
- **Corroboration structure**: Registry and land records established legal control; public photographs established relationships; multiple industry sources independently described the traffic pattern; site reporting verified the terminals’ role. The corruption interpretation rests on convergence, not on one proxy’s occupation alone ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire)).
- **Methodology notes**: No standalone about-the-data page was found. **[Inferred from the article]** Reporters constructed ownership timelines for each terminal, overlaid truck routing before/after each change, and enriched each short-tenure owner with employment, politics, family, and social links. The prior archive was used as a seed, not as the sole proof of current terminal control ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/how-to-build-a-customs-empire), [archive](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/saimaitis-archive)).
- **Official impact**: The broader Matraimov investigations were cited when Kyrgyz security services arrested Matraimov in October 2020; the United States later sanctioned him, and he pleaded guilty to corruption and paid about **$24.5 million** to the state. These are project-level impacts, not outcomes uniquely attributable to this article ([arrest](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/kyrgyz-authorities-arrest-raiymbek-matraimov), [U.S. sanctions](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/us-sanctions-matraimov-under-magnitsky-act), [guilty plea](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/kyrgyz-ex-customs-official-matraimov-pleads-guilty-to-graft-fined-3000)).
- **Generalization**: Apply to ports, bonded warehouses, inspection stations, payment gateways, landfill scales, public-health testing labs, and licensing agents. A detector looks for a fall in competitor traffic followed by rapid ownership transfer, public officials or political candidates entering the ownership chain, and one network’s facilities becoming mandatory despite equivalent licensed alternatives.

### The ‘Beautiful’ Life of a Kyrgyz Customs Official (2020) — Public posts and geolocation exposed assets and travel irreconcilable with declared public income

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-beautiful-life-of-a-kyrgyz-customs-official
- **Partner/awards**: Part of the explicit OCCRP–Kloop–Radio Azattyk–Bellingcat collaboration. OCCRP published two linked reporters’ notebooks showing the open-source geolocation work ([project credits](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom), [Dubai notebook](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-matraimovs-dubai-apartment), [Karven notebook](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-matraimovs-karven-cottage)).
- **What they found**:
  - Matraimov’s 2011–2017 declarations listed two Soviet-era apartments, but his wife’s posts showed the family using an Osh mansion and a **279-square-meter Bishkek penthouse**, both registered to Matraimov’s mother before passing to family members ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-beautiful-life-of-a-kyrgyz-customs-official)).
  - Reporters reviewed roughly **100 travel photographs** posted since 2015, reconstructing stays at five-star hotels, luxury travel, and later repeated use of a Dubai penthouse; comparable units sold for about **$1 million** ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-beautiful-life-of-a-kyrgyz-customs-official), [Dubai notebook](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-matraimovs-dubai-apartment)).
  - A domestic retreat was geolocated to a Karven Four Seasons cottage used repeatedly between 2015 and 2019, where VIP cottages cost about **$500 per night**; title was held by Matraimov’s sister-in-law ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-beautiful-life-of-a-kyrgyz-customs-official), [Karven notebook](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-matraimovs-karven-cottage)).
  - Prior leak-assisted reporting had separately identified at least **$12 million** in Dubai property purchases by Matraimov’s wife, reinforcing but not creating the open-source lifestyle mismatch ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-beautiful-life-of-a-kyrgyz-customs-official)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **declared-means/lived-assets divergence** — an official’s filed income and property are compared with observed family use; **kin-title parking** — assets used by an official’s household are legally held by a parent, sibling, or in-law; **social-post property attribution** — recurring interiors, views, household staff, and travel gaps establish use and apparent possession before title is known.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Asset and income declarations** — official filings for 2011–2017, obtained by reporters and used as the declared baseline ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-beautiful-life-of-a-kyrgyz-customs-official)).
  - **Property records** — title and inheritance histories for the Osh, Bishkek, and Issyk-Kul properties; obtained from land/property records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-beautiful-life-of-a-kyrgyz-customs-official)).
  - **Public social media** — posts by Uulkan Turgunova, a household employee, relatives, and associates; collected from Instagram, Facebook, and Odnoklassniki ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-beautiful-life-of-a-kyrgyz-customs-official)).
  - **Open-source geolocation** — skyline, window geometry, façade, floor height, interiors, and satellite imagery matched posts to Al Fattan Marine Towers and specific lakeside cottages ([Dubai notebook](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-matraimovs-dubai-apartment), [Karven notebook](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-matraimovs-karven-cottage)).
  - **Leaked Dubai property data** — used in the earlier $12 million finding; non-public at acquisition and not required for the newly geolocated penthouse or the domestic declaration diff ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-beautiful-life-of-a-kyrgyz-customs-official)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(a) open-record-runnable.** The decisive analytic move—declared means compared with property use—can run on declarations, property records, public posts, maps, satellite imagery, real-estate listings, and archived hotel/resort prices. The earlier Dubai leak increases scope and valuation, but removing it still leaves the undeclared-use and income-capacity findings.
- **Detection signature**: **declared-wealth lifestyle diff** — official asset declarations joined to title records on official/relative and property, then compared with geolocated family posts on interior, skyline, date, and recurring use, revealed high-value properties and travel absent from the declared household balance sheet.
- **Corroboration structure**: One post generated a lead; repeated posts from several accounts, matching interiors, household-employee statements in her own video, title histories, and comparable listings established location, use, and indicative value. OCCRP appropriately described the Dubai unit as “apparently owned” where legal title was not independently available ([Dubai notebook](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-matraimovs-dubai-apartment)).
- **Methodology notes**: OCCRP published step-by-step notebooks for the Dubai penthouse and Karven cottage, including the exact posts and visual features used to locate them ([Dubai notebook](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-matraimovs-dubai-apartment), [Karven notebook](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-matraimovs-karven-cottage)). **[Inferred boundary]** Repeated family use proves access and may establish beneficial enjoyment, but does not by itself prove legal ownership or illicit source of funds.
- **Official impact**: Matraimov had already been arrested and had agreed to compensate the state before this December article; the story therefore reports official status rather than claiming causation. Later proceedings recorded a guilty plea and approximately $24.5 million paid to the state ([story chronology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-matraimov-kingdom/the-beautiful-life-of-a-kyrgyz-customs-official), [plea](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/kyrgyz-ex-customs-official-matraimov-pleads-guilty-to-graft-fined-3000)).
- **Generalization**: Run against judges, customs officials, procurement officers, mayors, military commanders, and state-enterprise executives. The generic detector resolves relatives, collects declarations and title, clusters repeated interiors and locations from public posts, and flags assets whose acquisition/use window overlaps public tenure and exceeds plausible household income.

### Money for Nothing: South Africa Paid a Firm Millions for Pretending to Manage Its Properties (2020) — Invoices preceded approval for services OCCRP could not find evidence were performed

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties
- **Partner/awards**: OCCRP publication by Khadija Sharife and Mark Anderson. The State Capture Papers page says OCCRP exclusively obtained the Trillian server; no external coordinator, ICIJ role, Forbidden Stories role, or story-specific award is stated ([project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers), [story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
- **What they found**:
  - Transnet paid Trillian the equivalent of **$2.8 million** in 2016 for property-management services, although OCCRP found no evidence Trillian or its two front companies managed the properties ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
  - The proposal would move nearly **150 properties worth over 4 billion rand ($275 million)** and producing **610 million rand ($41.8 million)** annually into a private trust; the private managers would take **27.5 percent** of income ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
  - Trillian issued its first invoice on **April 15, 2016**, the same day it formally submitted the proposal and before formal approval; Transnet had attached the unrelated arrangement to a $1.5 billion locomotive deal ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
  - Avren joined unsolicited bids before it was incorporated and appeared to lack other business; the supposedly valuable management model was valued internally at **one rand** ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **pre-authorization billing** — payment begins before a proposal is approved or a contract exists; **service-without-performance** — invoices and fees have no corresponding operational output; **front-company procurement insertion** — purportedly independent suppliers are controlled by the bidder’s own associates.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Trillian server** — internal emails, proposals, PowerPoints, confidential invoices, and legal advice; exclusively obtained by OCCRP, not a public record source ([project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers), [story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
  - **Corporate records** — Avren’s incorporation date and proxy links; public/company-registry verification of internal claims ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
  - **Procurement law and tender notices** — requirements for public competition and the later tender with no announced winner; open legal/administrative records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
  - **State Capture Commission testimony** — Lynne Brown’s sworn account used to test what the ministry knew; public inquiry record ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(c) leak-dependent.** The decisive invoice/proposal chronology, proxy instructions, internal one-rand valuation, and evidence of no intended performance came from the Trillian server. **Nearest public substitute**: South African company records, Transnet procurement notices and annual accounts, the State Capture Commission record, and litigation/inquiry exhibits could identify Avren’s late formation, missing tender, and payments in aggregate. They lose the contemporaneous emails, exact invoices, internal valuation, and proof that insiders coordinated the front companies.
- **Detection signature**: **invoice-authorization chronology** — internal invoices and emails compared with proposal, approval, incorporation, tender, and payment dates on project/vendor/date revealed billing beginning before legal authorization and a vendor incorporated only after entering the bid.
- **Corroboration structure**: Internal documents supplied both the seller and buyer sides; registry dates tested corporate existence; governing law tested procurement legality; commission testimony and right-of-reply tested official knowledge. “No service” was framed as no evidence found rather than proof of metaphysical non-performance ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
- **Methodology notes**: OCCRP’s project page says the copied 2012–2017 server held **more than three million documents** and that the stories followed months of analysis; the article narrows this to tens of thousands of relevant emails plus invoices and attachments ([project methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers), [story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)). **[Inferred]** A robust implementation would extract every document event—draft, submission, approval, invoice, payment, tender—and test chronological and legal prerequisites.
- **Official impact**: The named Trillian and Gupta transactions were already subjects of South African government investigations and the State Capture Commission. No separate official outcome caused by this July 2020 article was identified, so ongoing proceedings are not presented as story impact ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-state-capture-papers/money-for-nothing-south-africa-paid-a-firm-millions-for-pretending-to-manage-its-properties)).
- **Generalization**: Search invoices, purchase orders, amendments, approvals, and vendor formation dates in any public entity. A generic detector flags invoices before proposal/approval, unrelated contracts used as vehicles, vendors created after work began, extreme fees for previously internal functions, and payments without staffing, deliverables, permits, or operational footprints.

### The 700-Million-Dollar Man (2019) — A money mover’s archive exposed a customs-protected cargo and transfer system spanning 12 countries

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/the-700-million-dollar-man
- **Partner/awards**: Joint RFE/RL, OCCRP, and **Kloop** reporting; OCCRP explicitly calls Kloop its Kyrgyz member center. The project won the **2019 IRE Tom Renner Award** and an Egizbaev investigative prize ([project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia), [award announcement](https://www.occrp.org/en/announcement/occrp-and-partners-from-kyrgyzstan-win-top-us-award-for-crime-reporting)).
- **What they found**:
  - Aierken Saimaiti said he moved at least **$700 million** in wire transfers and cash from Kyrgyzstan to roughly a dozen countries over five years, largely for the Abdukadyr network; Kyrgyz financial police separately reported hundreds of millions wired through his company and wife ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/the-700-million-dollar-man)).
  - Five source-provided sales contracts called for **$114 million** in goods in one year, while the bazaar supposedly receiving them had total turnover of only **$16.5 million across six years**—a paper-versus-capacity mismatch ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/the-700-million-dollar-man)).
  - Saimaiti described roughly **600 Abu Sahiy truck trips per month** and alleged bribes of $4,600–$5,000 per truck, or about **$3 million monthly**; current and former customs officials, a former minister, legislators, traders, and a driver gave convergent accounts of the system ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/the-700-million-dollar-man)).
  - His ledger listed nearly **$2.4 million** sent to a Matraimov family charity. Two original bank slips confirmed two ledger entries totaling **$200,000**, while other screenshots supported additional wires ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/the-700-million-dollar-man)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **insider-ledger re-anchoring** — a participant’s transaction table is decomposed into rows and checked against independent records; **trade-cover capacity mismatch** — contract volume exceeds the operational capacity of the purported buyer; **customs-patronage revenue share** — officials allegedly receive systematic, tiered payments in return for misclassification and protected passage.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Saimaiti archive** — ledgers, 843 deduplicated transaction records, invoices, bank slips, sham loan and sales contracts, and interviews; insider-supplied and later released with redactions ([archive](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/saimaitis-archive)).
  - **Corporate registries** — company trails in China, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, the UAE, Germany, the UK, and the US; independently obtained public/requestable records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/the-700-million-dollar-man)).
  - **Official records** — Kyrgyz financial-police figures, a Kazakh court ruling on improper cargo paperwork, government agreements, and public asset declarations ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/the-700-million-dollar-man)).
  - **Operational testimony and site reporting** — officials, customs officers, business competitors, drivers, and visits to bazaars and terminals; reporter-obtained corroboration ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/the-700-million-dollar-man)).
  - **Property data** — a leaked Dubai database plus public property/company information tied the Matraimov and Abdukadyr families to a joint development ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/the-700-million-dollar-man)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(b) re-anchoring.** The source archive was indispensable for discovery and for the complete transaction map. The public/reusable verification half included the financial-police outflow total, company registrations, the Kazakh cargo-document judgment, asset declarations, government agreements, and property records. These independently verify scale, recipients, shell capacity, and trade mechanics, although they do not reproduce every wire or prove the full $700 million attribution without Saimaiti’s records. OCCRP has since published the source archive, making audit possible but not converting its origin into an independent public record ([archive methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/saimaitis-archive)).
- **Detection signature**: **insider-ledger/public-record reconciliation** — insider transaction and contract rows joined to financial-police totals, bank confirmations, corporate registrations, property records, and court findings on company, amount, date, signatory, and stated goods revealed which transfers were independently anchored and where paper trade dwarfed real business capacity.
- **Corroboration structure**: OCCRP removed duplicate transactions, verified sums with public property information and newly obtained U.S. bank records, cross-checked contracts against turnover, and sought multiple witnesses at different operational levels. It preserved unverified elements and described the source as a self-confessed money launderer with interests of his own ([archive](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/saimaitis-archive), [story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/the-700-million-dollar-man)).
- **Methodology notes**: OCCRP’s archive page specifies **843 transactions**, duplicate removal, minor redactions, downloadable source records, the public-record checks, and subsequent U.S. bank confirmation; it is a genuine about-the-data page rather than an inferred workflow ([archive](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/saimaitis-archive)).
- **Official impact**: The reporting led to protests, and Prime Minister Mukhammedkalyi Abylgaziev requested an investigation into customs corruption. The United States later sanctioned Matraimov; Kyrgyz proceedings produced a guilty plea and roughly $24.5 million in compensation ([award/impact announcement](https://www.occrp.org/en/announcement/occrp-and-partners-from-kyrgyzstan-win-top-us-award-for-crime-reporting), [sanctions](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/us-sanctions-matraimov-under-magnitsky-act), [plea](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/kyrgyz-ex-customs-official-matraimov-pleads-guilty-to-graft-fined-3000)).
- **Generalization**: Normalize any insider archive into claims with `source_claimed`, `documented`, `publicly_confirmed`, and `contradicted` amounts. Join rows to bank confirmations, registries, tax/turnover filings, court cases, customs decisions, and assets; publish totals by verification state rather than allowing one charismatic source to authenticate the whole corpus.

### A Real Estate Empire Built on Dark Money (2019) — Wires from Kyrgyzstan reappeared as at least 20 properties across four countries

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/a-real-estate-empire-built-on-dark-money
- **Partner/awards**: Part of the Tom Renner-winning joint project by OCCRP, RFE/RL’s Radio Azattyk, and member center Kloop, with jurisdictional reporting across Germany, the UK, the US, and Dubai ([project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia), [award](https://www.occrp.org/en/announcement/occrp-and-partners-from-kyrgyzstan-win-top-us-award-for-crime-reporting)).
- **What they found**:
  - Saimaiti’s records showed at least **$75 million** wired to EU accounts, **$31 million** to Bank of America accounts, and **$104 million** to Dubai accounts or developers. These totals tracked closely with Kyrgyz financial-police country totals ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/a-real-estate-empire-built-on-dark-money)).
  - Land and company records identified at least **20 properties**. Known purchase value was at least **$65 million**: roughly $44 million in the UK, $19 million in Dubai, and $2 million in the US, excluding incomplete German and development costs ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/a-real-estate-empire-built-on-dark-money)).
  - UK filings showed Miran International received about **$28 million** from two foreign Abdukadyr companies in 2016 and used a corresponding amount for real estate, including Dawley House. German filings showed two group companies held over **$106 million** in 2017 despite a largely empty headquarters and stalled projects ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/a-real-estate-empire-built-on-dark-money)).
  - A purported **$30 million loan agreement** provided trade cover; seven wire orders totaling **$3.7 million** referred to it. In Dubai, property identifiers embedded in “textile” wire descriptions matched identifiers in the leaked property dataset ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/a-real-estate-empire-built-on-dark-money)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **flow-to-asset conversion** — suspicious transfers are matched to durable asset acquisition; **purpose-code contradiction** — payment text claims goods while the recipient and property identifier indicate real estate; **phantom-development parking** — capital accumulates in land and development companies whose announced projects or headquarters show little operating substance.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Source-provided wires and contracts** — Saimaiti’s transaction spreadsheet, transfer orders, sham loans, and contracts; insider material later released by OCCRP ([archive](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/saimaitis-archive)).
  - **Land/deed and permitting records** — title, purchase values, planning applications, and parcel histories in the UK and US; public or requestable local records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/a-real-estate-empire-built-on-dark-money), [published UK ownership records](https://www.occrp.org/documents/plunder-and-patronage/UKProperties.pdf)).
  - **Corporate filings and financial statements** — Companies House and German company accounts established owners, intercompany funding, assets, and operating capacity ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/a-real-estate-empire-built-on-dark-money)).
  - **Dubai property database** — compiled by real-estate professionals, obtained by C4ADS, and provided to OCCRP; non-public at acquisition ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/a-real-estate-empire-built-on-dark-money)).
  - **Shipping/import records and site visits** — US imports of construction materials, furniture, and luxury vehicles plus physical checks of addresses and development sites; public commercial records and constructed reporting ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/a-real-estate-empire-built-on-dark-money)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(b) re-anchoring.** Discovery and exact source-of-funds attribution depended on Saimaiti’s wires; Dubai ownership also used a leak. The public reusable half is unusually strong: UK/US deeds, Companies House filings, German accounts, planning records, imports, virtual-office addresses, and site conditions independently reconstruct the acquisition and shell network. That half finds the assets and capacity mismatch; it loses the continuous Kyrgyzstan-to-purchase money trail and most Dubai title detail.
- **Detection signature**: **wire-to-property reappearance join** — source transfer rows joined to corporate funding and deeds on beneficiary company, amount band, date, and property, with wire purpose text compared to recipient business and parcel identifier, revealed “textile” or “loan” payments reappearing as land, homes, and stalled developments.
- **Corroboration structure**: Bank records established transfers; public company accounts showed corresponding capital and purchases; deeds fixed legal title; leaked Dubai identifiers connected specific units; site visits tested whether declared headquarters and developments existed. OCCRP separated known purchase value from incomplete total wealth ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/a-real-estate-empire-built-on-dark-money)).
- **Methodology notes**: The [Saimaiti archive](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia/saimaitis-archive) explains the source files, deduplication, confirmations, and downloads. **[Inferred from the property story]** Reporters resolved every wire recipient to a corporate family, then searched property title, accounts, permits, imports, archived company websites, and physical addresses in each jurisdiction.
- **Official impact**: The project-level protests, customs investigation request, U.S. sanctions, and Kyrgyz guilty plea apply here; no separate property forfeiture was identified as a direct result of this article ([award/impact announcement](https://www.occrp.org/en/announcement/occrp-and-partners-from-kyrgyzstan-win-top-us-award-for-crime-reporting), [sanctions](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/us-sanctions-matraimov-under-magnitsky-act)).
- **Generalization**: Apply to contractors, customs brokers, state-bank borrowers, and sanctioned networks. A generic detector joins suspicious inflows to company capital, mortgages, deeds, permits, aircraft, vehicles, or token transfers within a bounded time window and flags misleading memo text, newly formed buyers, phantom headquarters, and asset values disproportionate to filed operations.

### Azerbaijani First Family Big on Banking (2015) — Public bank reports placed $3 billion in assets under first-family and insider influence

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking
- **Partner/awards**: OCCRP-hosted and credited simply to OCCRP. One supporting offshore-company link came from earlier ICIJ work, but the article is not presented as an ICIJ-coordinated project; no Forbidden Stories role or story-specific award is stated ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
- **What they found**:
  - Aliyev family members and close advisers were significant shareholders in at least **eight banks controlling more than $3 billion in assets**, equal to **19 percent of Azerbaijan’s banking assets** at the start of 2014 ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
  - Pasha, Kapital, and Xalq were among the four largest private banks. Public bank statements or audits identified daughters Leyla and Arzu Aliyeva as ultimate owners/controllers of Pasha and Kapital and “L. Aliyeva” as Xalq’s ultimate controlling party ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
  - Ata Holding was 75 percent owned through companies listing Leyla and Arzu as officers and the president’s son Heydar as ultimate controller; Ata held bank shares and interests across telecommunications, construction, transportation, mining, oil, and gas ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
  - At the same time, **2.3 million people** held bank loans and average consumer rates were about **18 percent**, creating a captured-economy loop in which consumers could borrow from family-linked banks to buy from family-linked companies ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **elite-sector concentration** — one political family’s disclosed and proxy holdings are aggregated across an economically strategic sector; **related-market enclosure** — the same network controls consumer credit and major providers of goods/services; **address-based ownership bridge** — a company’s registered address links its nominal controller to a political family.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Audited bank financial statements** — Pasha, Kapital, Xalq, Expressbank, and other reports identifying shareholders, ultimate controllers, and asset totals; publicly released bank disclosures ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
  - **Holding-company and corporate records** — Ata, Pasha, Gilan, ADOR, United Enterprises, and shareholder companies; public/historical corporate records, though OCCRP noted Azerbaijan had restricted commercial-registry access in 2012 ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
  - **Address co-registration** — ADOR shared an address with Mehriban, Leyla, and Arzu Aliyeva; registry/address evidence used as a relationship lead, not alone as beneficial-ownership proof ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
  - **Central-bank sector data and macro statistics** — bank asset shares, borrower count, interest rates, inflation, and exchange-rate changes; official/public statistics ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
  - **ICIJ offshore-company reporting** — supporting evidence for one connected industrial-bank family, not the decisive dataset for the eight-bank concentration ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(a) open-record-runnable.** The decisive ownership and concentration calculation came from public bank reports, audits, holding-company records, and central-bank totals. Current Azerbaijani registry restrictions make refreshes harder, but an outside investigator can rerun the method using archived and current bank statements, regulator disclosures, OpenCorporates-style aggregators, address history, and public Offshore Leaks data. No private transaction ledger is required.
- **Detection signature**: **beneficial-owner sector concentration** — bank financial statements and audits joined to holding-company ownership on ultimate controller, shareholder, and reporting date, then divided by central-bank sector assets, revealed one ruling-family network controlling at least eight banks and 19 percent of sector assets.
- **Corroboration structure**: Multiple independent auditors named ultimate controllers; presidential spokespeople confirmed some holdings; holding-company records connected banks to the children and in-laws; central-bank totals supplied the denominator. Address overlap was corroborative only ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
- **Methodology notes**: No standalone methods page was found. **[Inferred from the article’s bank-by-bank inventory]** Normalize every bank’s year-end assets and ultimate owner from the latest comparable audited statement, resolve ownership through holding companies, avoid double counting cross-holdings, and calculate both family-only and family-plus-associate shares against one central-bank denominator.
- **Official impact**: No official divestiture, enforcement action, or registry reform attributable to this June 2015 article was identified. The article itself notes that a 2005 asset-declaration law was not enforced and that commercial-registry access had already been restricted ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-first-family-big-on-banking)).
- **Generalization**: Run for banking, telecom, construction, insurance, media, pharmaceuticals, or extractives. A generic detector resolves political families and associates through audited “ultimate controlling party” notes, aggregates assets/revenue/licenses by beneficial network, and tests whether the same network also receives public deposits, contracts, or regulatory appointments.

### Aliyevs’ Secret Mining Empire (2016) — Panama Papers ownership records showed the president’s daughters controlled 56 percent of a privileged gold consortium

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire
- **Partner/awards**: **ICIJ-organized Panama Papers collaboration**. Süddeutsche Zeitung obtained Mossack Fonseca’s documents and ICIJ shared them with OCCRP and more than 100 media partners; OCCRP published this article, and RFE/RL contributed reporting. This is not an OCCRP-coordinated leak and has no Forbidden Stories role ([Panama Papers project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers), [story attribution](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)).
- **What they found**:
  - President Ilham Aliyev had awarded six gold fields worth billions to a consortium of one UK and three offshore companies. Earlier reporting linked his daughters to an 11 percent share; Mossack Fonseca files revealed their control of another 45 percent through Londex Resources, bringing control to **56 percent** ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)).
  - The consortium spent nearly **$230 million** and produced up to **$30 million in gold** before stopping. About **300 workers** were left unpaid for nearly two years while remaining formally employed and unable to take other jobs ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)).
  - The unpublished concession gave the consortium **70 percent of profits**, compared with 49 percent under an earlier mining agreement, and OCCRP could find no evidence of a tender ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)).
  - The consortium failed to pay a promised **$2 million bonus** and faced a **$2.8 million penalty**, yet auditors said the government never demanded payment; non-payment would have allowed termination ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **self-dealing concession** — a public resource award benefits the awarding official’s concealed family interests; **offshore ownership peel-back** — successive legal layers are resolved to political beneficiaries; **enforcement immunity for insiders** — contractual defaults that permit termination go unenforced against a ruling-family asset.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Mossack Fonseca records** — shareholder/control documents for Londex and related offshore companies; leaked to Süddeutsche Zeitung and shared by ICIJ ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire), [project methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers)).
  - **Government and parliamentary records** — the concession award, parliamentary debate, profit split, lease term, and absence of a visible tender; official/public proceedings, although the full agreement was unpublished ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)).
  - **Financial statements and audit records** — $230 million spent, $146 million borrowed, unpaid bonus, penalty, and related-family bank loans; obtained financial records and audits ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)).
  - **Labor/court and ministry records** — worker emails, legal filings, and unanswered petitions to ministries; source-provided and public litigation/administrative material ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)).
  - **Worker/executive interviews and site reporting** — miners, former CEO Carl Caumartin, and mining executives; reporter-obtained and physically checked at Chovdar ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(c) leak-dependent.** The decisive hidden 45 percent—and thus the 56 percent family control of the concession—came from full Mossack Fonseca records. **Nearest public substitute**: ICIJ’s public Offshore Leaks Database, UK company records for Globex, parliamentary concession records, AIMROC financial statements, and current ownership aggregators. They can recreate entity names, public share percentages, favorable concession terms, and operational failure, but may lose the internal shareholder/control instruments needed to prove the daughters’ Londex interest at the relevant date.
- **Detection signature**: **concession-to-hidden-owner conflict join** — public concession partners and percentage interests joined to offshore provider records on company, shareholder, intermediary, and date revealed that the awarding president’s daughters controlled 56 percent of the favored consortium.
- **Corroboration structure**: The leak resolved beneficial control; an earlier independent RFE/RL–OCCRP investigation had already linked the daughters to the UK member; parliamentary records proved the public award and unusual terms; audits showed non-enforcement and related-bank funding; workers and site visits established harm ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/aliyevs-secret-mining-empire)).
- **Methodology notes**: OCCRP’s [Panama Papers project page](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers) states source provenance and ICIJ’s coordinating role and links to the public database. **[Inferred for this story]** Reporters began with concession counterparties, resolved every ownership layer in the leak, then compared the resulting family stake with tender history, concession terms, financing, production, and enforcement.
- **Official impact**: On May 25, 2016, President Aliyev ordered state-owned AzerGold to acquire the four-company consortium, including the two companies OCCRP and earlier reporting linked to his daughters. This was an official state takeover, though the terms and causal relationship to the April story were unclear ([follow-up](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-panama-papers/people-of-azerbaijan-bail-out-failed-aliyev-mining-empire)).
- **Generalization**: Apply to mines, oil blocks, timber concessions, spectrum, casinos, fisheries, and privatizations. A detector resolves every concession partner through public and offshore ownership, intersects beneficiaries with the awarding official’s family/associate graph, and compares economics and enforcement with peer concessions.

### Azerbaijani Insiders Benefited from Currency Collapse (2016) — Family-linked banks and companies repositioned immediately before devaluation and gained at least $64 million

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse
- **Partner/awards**: OCCRP publication reported by Dave Bloss, Miranda Patrucic, Khadija Ismayilova, and OCCRP Azerbaijan. No external coordinator, ICIJ project role, Forbidden Stories role, or story-specific award is stated ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
- **What they found**:
  - Two transactions by the Aliyev family’s network and close associate Ashraf Kamilov produced at least **100 million manat ($64 million)** around the 2015 devaluations, while the banking sector lost over **$1 billion** and four banks lost licenses ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
  - Synergy bought a nearly inactive bank in late December 2014, recapitalized it, obtained a renewed license on January 26, and three days later lent state oil company SOCAR **$52 million in dollars**. Three weeks later the first devaluation produced a 13 million manat gain; after the second, the loan’s gain reached **39.2 million manat** ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
  - On February 19—**two days before devaluation**—Synergy company AAC repaid 60 million manat and immediately re-borrowed a slightly larger amount in dollars, despite stating it had no foreign-exchange exposure. AAC gained about **20 million manat**, while AtaBank’s dollar lending generated **61 million manat** ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
  - AtaBank’s own audit said insider lending reached **240 percent of statutory capital** against a 20 percent rule; one related party received 91 percent of loans against a 10 percent limit, and collateral coverage was 28 percent against a 100–150 percent requirement ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **event-eve currency positioning** — a related party changes currency exposure immediately before an official devaluation; **captured-regulator tolerance** — extreme, disclosed limit breaches produce plans rather than meaningful enforcement; **state-company value transfer** — a liquid state enterprise accepts expensive financing from a small insider-linked bank.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Bank annual reports and independent audits** — loan currency, related parties, capital, collateral, foreign-exchange gains, and violations; public financial disclosures ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
  - **Holding-company records** — ownership of Caspian Development Bank, AtaBank, Pasha Bank, Synergy, and AAC; public/historical corporate and bank disclosures ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
  - **Central-bank and executive records** — license date, exchange-rate decisions, prudential limits, and creation/appointments of the new Financial Market Supervisory Board; official/public records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
  - **SOCAR financial statements** — showed roughly $1.8 billion in cash and equivalents, including more than $1 billion in dollars, testing whether it needed the $52 million loan; public company accounts ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
  - **Reporter calculations** — before/after currency conversion and profit reconstruction from filed amounts and official rates; constructed analysis ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(a) open-record-runnable.** The decisive dates, loan currencies, related parties, prudential limits, exchange rates, and profits came from public bank/holding reports and official decisions. An outside investigator can rebuild the event window without transaction leaks, subject to continued availability of archived reports.
- **Detection signature**: **event-window balance-sheet diff** — year-end and transaction disclosures compared with devaluation dates on borrower, lender, currency, amount, and restructuring date revealed insider-linked entities switching into dollar assets days before the manat fell and capturing gains unavailable to ordinary borrowers.
- **Corroboration structure**: Ownership reports established the family network; audited accounts supplied the pre-event positions and gains; central-bank records fixed event time and legal limits; SOCAR accounts falsified an obvious liquidity-need explanation. The article framed advance knowledge as a question because timing and benefit do not alone prove a tip ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
- **Methodology notes**: No standalone methods page was found. **[Inferred from the article]** Normalize all related-party loans into original and post-devaluation manat values; calculate counterfactual results absent the currency switch; compare each position change with public-policy announcement times and the entity’s stated currency-risk policy.
- **Official impact**: Azerbaijan created a new Financial Market Supervisory Board after the crisis, but the story notes that its CEO previously chaired Caspian Development Bank’s supervisory board. No post-publication enforcement attributable to the article was found ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/corruptistan-azerbaijan/azerbaijani-insiders-benefited-from-currency-collapse)).
- **Generalization**: Apply to devaluations, rate changes, sanctions, tax changes, bank resolutions, zoning, privatizations, and emergency orders. A detector compares related-party asset/liability currency and maturity snapshots in the 1–90 days before a non-public or surprise state event, then contrasts profits with peers and declared risk policy.

### Montenegro: Buying up Paradise (Part 2) (2014) — Insider lending, criminal collateral, and a €44 million bailout socialized an elite coastal gamble

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2
- **Partner/awards**: OCCRP story by Miranda Patrucic with contributions from **Dejan Milovac (MANS)**, **Stevan Dojčinović (CINS)**, and Lejla Camdžić (OCCRP); the series was published by OCCRP and regional outlets including Vijesti, Dan, Delo, and Oslobođenje. Unholy Alliances won the **2015 Global Shining Light Award** and was nominated for the European Press Prize; OCCRP’s award record preserves CINS in the story-era credit while later identifying Dojčinović with KRIK ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2), [award record](https://www.occrp.org/en/feature/occrp-and-member-center-awards), [Global Shining Light announcement](https://www.occrp.org/en/announcement/occrp-wins-global-shining-light-award)).
- **What they found**:
  - Stanko Subotić borrowed almost **€21 million** from the Đukanović family-controlled First Bank to buy coastal property, becoming its largest debtor; loans were often weakly secured, exceeded legal concentration limits, and allowed lump-sum maturity payments ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2)).
  - Seven secret Central Bank/PwC reports showed San Investments was new, lacked income and a business plan, and had more loans than First Bank disclosed to examiners. The bank knew exposure exceeded **25 percent of its capital** and concealed it ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2)).
  - A company owned by accused cocaine traffickers Darko and Duško Šarić supplied **€4.2 million** in collateral for Subotić. The same records tied elite borrowers, organized crime, and a ruling-family bank into one liquidity system ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2)).
  - After bad loans and depositor withdrawals, First Bank sought and received a **€44 million state liquidity loan** while owing €32 million to creditors and depositors; subsequent offshore ownership changes left the island’s ultimate owner unknown and the development unfinished ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **elite-credit privilege** — politically connected borrowers receive terms unavailable to ordinary borrowers; **criminal-collateral bridge** — an organized-crime-linked company supplies collateral to a politically connected debtor; **private-risk/public-rescue cascade** — insider lending failure leads to a taxpayer liquidity rescue and opaque asset transfer.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Seven Central Bank/PwC examination reports** — loan files, collateral, concentration breaches, liquidity, and regulatory orders; secret reports obtained by OCCRP ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2)).
  - **Internal bank records** — loan and deposit agreements, collateral substitutions, debt splits, and account information; non-public documents obtained by reporters ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2)).
  - **Corporate and offshore records** — San Investments, Samuelson, Velo Business Services, Adriatic Overseas Holdings, and Lafino Trade; public/requestable records plus opaque BVI/Panama layers ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2)).
  - **Land, mortgage, auction, tender, and bailout records** — island acquisition, failed auctions, bank mortgage, Duvankomerc bid, and state liquidity loan; public administrative/property records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2)).
  - **Court and law-enforcement records** — Šarić/Subotić cases, Interpol status, and later litigation revealing beneficial deposit ownership; public judicial records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(c) leak-dependent.** The secret examiner reports and bank files were decisive for concentration breaches, concealment, collateral swaps, and the causal chain from privileged loan to insolvency. **Nearest public substitute**: corporate histories, land/mortgage records, auction notices, state bailout decisions, criminal dockets, and offshore databases. They can reconstruct the borrower network, property sequence, and public rescue but lose undisclosed loan count, examiner findings, false regulator statements, and the internal reason collateral was released or substituted.
- **Detection signature**: **loan-to-land-to-bailout reconstruction** — secret loan and examiner records joined to company ownership, parcel/mortgage events, criminal cases, and the state rescue on borrower, guarantor, property, amount, and date revealed private coastal speculation financed by a family bank and ultimately absorbed by taxpayers.
- **Corroboration structure**: Regulator reports documented rule breaches; internal agreements fixed amounts and collateral; corporate/land records proved legal transfers; court and law-enforcement records established the crime-network status; bailout records fixed public exposure. The unresolved final owner is recorded as a gap, not filled by inference ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-buying-up-paradise-part-2)).
- **Methodology notes**: No standalone methods page was found. **[Inferred from the article]** Reporters built a dated state machine for every loan, deposit, collateral item, property title, auction, offshore transfer, and regulatory order, then tested whether claimed repayment changed mortgage/title and whether public money entered after private collateral exited.
- **Official impact**: Within a week of the series, MANS asked Montenegro’s Special Prosecutor for Organized Crime and Corruption to investigate a dozen officials and First Bank. OCCRP did not report an enforcement outcome in that notice ([formal complaints](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/montenegro-ngo-accuses-a-dozen-high-level-officials-of-corruption)).
- **Generalization**: Run on public-development banks, politically connected commercial banks, pension funds, and municipal land banks. A generic detector joins related-party loans to collateral/title, monitors collateral release despite delinquency, and measures how much state liquidity or guarantees enter after insiders withdraw deposits or transfer assets offshore.

### Montenegro: Prime Minister’s Family Bank Catered to Organized Crime (2014) — Regulator files connected a ruling-family bank’s best terms to convicted and accused traffickers

- **URL**: https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime
- **Partner/awards**: OCCRP story by Miranda Patrucic with contributions from **Stevan Dojčinović (CINS)** and **Dejan Milovac (MANS)**; project-level Global Shining Light and European Press Prize recognition as above. MANS is described by OCCRP as its Montenegrin member center in a later institutional statement; the article preserves the historical CINS label ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime), [MANS member-center statement](https://www.occrp.org/en/announcement/occrp-condemns-legal-action-against-its-member-center-in-montenegro), [award record](https://www.occrp.org/en/feature/occrp-and-member-center-awards)).
- **What they found**:
  - Over **1,000 pages** of internal bank and Central Bank records showed First Bank opened accounts, accepted deposits, and issued favorable loans to Darko Šarić’s companies and associates while repeatedly failing anti-money-laundering and internal controls ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime)).
  - Camarilla’s First Bank account was used to launder **€7.4 million**, according to the criminal case in which Duško Šarić was convicted; Lafino Trade made a **€6 million five-year deposit** and received only 1.5 percent interest while the bank was in a liquidity crisis ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime)).
  - First Bank never reported a suspicious transaction for Lafino or Camarilla, according to the former AML-agency head’s trial testimony, despite missing company/identity documents and prosecutors’ findings that both were used to launder money ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime)).
  - Mat Company received a **€5 million** loan plus €300,000 despite weak profitability; Trecom had at least **€1.2 million** outstanding. Both moved through Šarić associates or proxies while financial statements showed chronic losses ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **criminal-client bank capture** — a politically controlled bank becomes a stable service node for an organized-crime network; **KYC nullification** — mandatory identity and beneficial-owner checks are systematically absent for high-risk clients; **reciprocal liquidity** — a criminal network supplies cheap deposits while receiving loans, access, and tolerance.
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - **Central Bank reports and internal bank files** — accounts, deposits, KYC gaps, loans, collateral, rates, and bank violations; non-public records obtained by OCCRP ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime)).
  - **Corporate registries** — Delaware records for Lafino and related entities plus Montenegro company histories; public/requestable records used to resolve formal owners and proxies ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime)).
  - **Court, prosecution, and trial records** — cocaine and money-laundering cases, convictions, transfers, and AML-agency testimony; public judicial records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime)).
  - **Land and mortgage records** — collateral properties and related Budva transactions; public title/security records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime)).
  - **Financial statements** — borrower losses, liquidity, and capacity; company accounts compared against loans ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime)).
- **INPUT DEPENDENCY**: **(c) leak-dependent.** The central finding that Šarić was a top depositor/customer receiving exceptional treatment and no suspicious-transaction reports requires regulator and internal bank files. **Nearest public substitute**: Delaware/Montenegro corporate records, court judgments, prosecutor exhibits, mortgages, and bailout records can identify the network and some loans/transfers. They lose account rankings, rates, internal KYC failures, unreported suspicious activity, examiner conclusions, and the full reciprocal deposit–loan relationship.
- **Detection signature**: **regulator-file/criminal-docket join** — bank examiner accounts and loan rows joined to corporate ownership and criminal cases on company, beneficial owner, proxy, amount, account, and date revealed that convicted or accused traffickers were among a ruling-family bank’s most important and favorably treated clients.
- **Corroboration structure**: Regulator records showed what the bank knew and did; trial testimony established the absence of suspicious reports; criminal judgments established legal status and laundering use; Delaware records confirmed owners; borrower statements showed lack of repayment capacity. The criminal findings are attributed to prosecutors or courts rather than asserted independently ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/unholy-alliances/montenegro-prime-ministers-family-bank-catered-to-organized-crime)).
- **Methodology notes**: No standalone about-the-data page was found. **[Inferred from the story]** Create one table of bank relationships—account, deposit, loan, rate, collateral, KYC state, suspicious-report state—and a second time-aware criminal/corporate graph; join them at company and beneficial owner, then compare treatment with ordinary policy and peer depositors.
- **Official impact**: MANS’s post-series complaints asked the Special Prosecutor to examine First Bank and a dozen senior officials. No resulting prosecution was verified in the OCCRP follow-up used here ([formal complaints](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/montenegro-ngo-accuses-a-dozen-high-level-officials-of-corruption)).
- **Generalization**: Applicable to local banks, credit unions, state banks, crypto exchanges, trust companies, and money-service businesses. A detector joins known criminal/prosecution entities to disclosed borrowers, collateral, correspondent relationships, or regulatory actions and flags unusually low deposit rates, high unsecured credit, absent beneficial-owner files, and zero suspicious reports despite later adjudicated laundering.

### 4. Transferable pattern candidates

#### Pattern A — The Hidden Contractor / Proxy Stack

- **Mechanics**: Inventory public projects, permits, and sites; identify observed contractors from decrees, signs, broadcasts, worker posts, and payments; resolve corporate histories; enrich owners/directors with public appointments, relatives, past employers, political campaigns, addresses, and social ties; cluster vendors that converge on one patron.
- **Minimum data**: Project/site list, contractor or permit holder, company registry history, person-role graph, and at least one independent relationship source.
- **Recognition cues**: Contractor never publicly announced; company formed immediately before work; rapid owner/director changes; owners with no sector experience; the same workforce across supposedly competing firms; public-office employees moving through vendor directorships.
- **Boundary**: Friendship, hometown, or a shared worker is not beneficial control. Escalate only when several independent edges converge and procurement/process anomalies coexist.

#### Pattern B — Paper Promise / Economic Performance

- **Mechanics**: Model each contract, invoice, loan, declaration, concession, payment memo, or collateral document as a promised event. Join it to delivery, traffic, turnover, staffing, title, payment, production, and enforcement records and locate the missing or contradictory leg.
- **Minimum data**: A dated legal/financial instrument, parties and amount, plus one independent performance or capacity source.
- **Recognition cues**: Invoice before approval; “textile” payment to a developer; $114 million contract into a $16.5 million market; one-rand internal valuation for a highly priced service; unpaid concession bonus that triggers no termination.
- **Boundary**: Legitimate projects fail and invoices can precede final paperwork. Repetition, related parties, lack of capacity, and favorable enforcement make the pattern probative.

#### Pattern C — Insider Ledger Re-Anchoring

- **Mechanics**: Treat every insider row as a claim, not a fact. Normalize entity, account, amount, currency, date, purpose, and supporting document; deduplicate; join to bank confirmations, corporate and property records, official aggregate totals, court cases, and border/trade records; publish verified and unverified subsets separately.
- **Minimum data**: Insider claim table plus at least two independent public or requestable record systems.
- **Recognition cues**: Independent totals track the source ledger; signatures or property IDs recur across source and registry documents; claimed counterparties receive matching capital/property; sham contracts exceed recipient capacity.
- **Boundary**: A public record can confirm identity or amount without confirming criminal purpose. Preserve separate verification fields for occurrence, ownership, source of funds, and illicit interpretation.

#### Pattern D — Event-Eve Insider Positioning

- **Mechanics**: Build a time series of loans, currencies, ownership, collateral, tenders, invoices, and asset transfers; align it with devaluations, regulatory decisions, sanctions, elections, bailouts, or policy exemptions; calculate insider gain and peer/citizen loss under the event.
- **Minimum data**: Reliable event timestamp, dated position changes, beneficial ownership, and amounts sufficient for a counterfactual.
- **Recognition cues**: Currency conversion days before devaluation; new license followed by a huge state-company deal; invoice on proposal day; collateral released before public rescue; corporate transfer immediately after a regulatory order.
- **Boundary**: Timing does not prove advance knowledge. Test ordinary business explanations, compare with peer behavior, and distinguish profit from privileged information.

#### Pattern E — Private Risk / Public Rescue Cascade

- **Mechanics**: Join insider lending or concession awards to collateral and asset transfers; track defaults, regulator warnings, enforcement, and withdrawals; measure later state loans, guarantees, purchases, or assumption of liabilities; identify which private parties retained assets or escaped repayment.
- **Minimum data**: Loan/concession terms, collateral/title, regulator or audit findings, default dates, and public-rescue transaction.
- **Recognition cues**: Concentrated related-party credit; collateral not seized; insiders withdraw deposits; bailout follows; offshore ownership changes at a repeated price; state purchases a failed family venture despite contractual termination rights.
- **Boundary**: A rescue can protect depositors or essential services. The detector must separate public-interest stabilization from selective preservation of insider value.

Immediate high-value implementations are therefore:

1. **US contractor proxy graph** — USASpending/FPDS awardees → registry owners/directors → OpenSanctions/PEP ties → property, FAA, USPTO, and CourtListener enrichment.
2. **Offshore concession conflict screen** — ICIJ Offshore Leaks entities → public officials/families in OpenSanctions → domestic companies and assets in registry/deed tools.
3. **Declared-capacity vendor screen** — contract value compared with company age, filings, staff/addresses where available, patents, litigation, and asset footprint.
4. **Flow-to-asset re-anchor** — any public ledger, court exhibit, or attributed blockchain address joined to companies, deeds, aircraft, and subsequent transfers.
5. **Event-window watchlist** — ownership, asset, award, court, sanctions, and on-chain changes in a bounded window before/after a public policy or enforcement event.

The largest missing layer is not another global company aggregator. It is **versioned local administrative data**: corporate ownership history, land parcels, procurement decisions, customs declarations, public-bank loans, municipal resolutions, asset declarations, regulator examination findings, and local-language archives. Local OpenAleph can organize documents already held, but it does not substitute for Aleph Pro’s private corpus or for records that were never obtained.
