# OCCRP Laundromat / Banking-Leak Canon: Evidence Ontology

## Scope and verification

This intake treats an OCCRP-coordinated project as the unit, followed by two finding-thread sub-entries. The canon verified here contains ten projects: **The Proxy Platform** (2011), **The Russian Laundromat** (2014, expanded with bank records in 2017), **The Azerbaijani Laundromat** (2017), **The Troika Laundromat** (2019), **Plunder and Patronage in the Heart of Central Asia** (2019), **The Riviera Maya Gang** (2020), **Suisse Secrets** (2022), **NarcoFiles** (2023), **Cyprus Confidential** (2023), and **Dubai Unlocked** (2024). OCCRP itself identifies Proxy, Russian, Azerbaijani, and Troika as its four named laundromats as of 2020; the other six are included as adjacent banking-leak, criminal-flow, or asset-leak projects that reuse the same evidence mechanics ([OCCRP laundromat FAQ](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/laundromats-explained-how-shell-companies-are-used-to-launder-money/frequently-asked-questions)).

Three candidate-list corrections matter. First, OCCRP’s Central Asian **Plunder and Patronage** project and its **Riviera Maya Gang** project are separate investigations with different sources and partner teams ([Plunder project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/plunder-and-patronage-in-the-heart-of-central-asia); [Riviera Maya project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/how-a-crew-of-romanian-criminals-conquered-the-world-of-atm-skimming)). Second, NarcoFiles was built by OCCRP, CLIP, Vorágine, Cerosetenta/070, and more than 40 partners from data supplied by Distributed Denial of Secrets and Enlace Hacktivista; it was **not** a Forbidden Stories collaboration ([NarcoFiles project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/narcofiles-the-new-criminal-order); [NarcoFiles FAQ](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/narcofiles-the-new-criminal-order/what-is-narcofiles-the-new-criminal-order-everything-you-need-to-know)). Third, Cyprus Confidential was led by ICIJ and Paper Trail Media. Its entries below cover only OCCRP reporting and OCCRP-held/shared leak components, not the global project’s data engineering, to avoid double-counting an ICIJ extraction ([Cyprus Confidential project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/cyprus-confidential)).

FinCEN Files, Panama Papers, Paradise Papers, and Pandora Papers are not separately entered because they are ICIJ-led projects rather than OCCRP-coordinated units. Dubai’s Golden Sands (2018) and Dubai Uncovered (2022) are treated as methodological predecessors to Dubai Unlocked, not independent entries, because the 2024 project is the latest coordinated property-leak unit and explicitly builds on the same ownership-screening logic ([Dubai’s Golden Sands](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/dubais-golden-sands); [Dubai Uncovered](https://www.occrp.org/en/investigation/dubai-uncovered-data-leak-exposes-how-criminals-officials-and-sanctioned-politicians-poured-money-into-dubai-real-estate); [Dubai Unlocked FAQ](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/dubai-unlocked/what-is-dubai-unlocked-everything-you-need-to-know)).

## Canon entries

### PROJECT — The Proxy Platform (2011) — Three nominally separate shell companies exposed a reusable international payment platform
- **URL**: [OCCRP project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform); [main investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/the-proxy-platform).
- **Partner/awards** (member centers/lead outlets; prizes): OCCRP coordinated reporters from Novaya Gazeta, Re:Baltica/TV3 Latvia, Kyiv Post, CINS, CIN Bosnia and Herzegovina, and other member centers ([project team](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/about-us)). OCCRP lists the project as a 2013 Daniel Pearl Award finalist ([awards](https://www.occrp.org/en/about-us/awards)).
- **What they found** (2-4 concrete bullets — names, amounts, scale):
  - Bank records for Tormex, Sirena, and Nomirex showed thousands of transactions among phantom companies with no genuine offices, employees, or tax activity but recurring counterparties and proxy directors ([main investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/the-proxy-platform)).
  - Tormex alone moved roughly **$680 million in 15 months**; the platform touched actors tied to the Magnitsky fraud, Asian organized crime, Moldovan business interests, and entities in the Wachovia/Sinaloa money-laundering case ([main investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/the-proxy-platform)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **shell-network convergence** (nominally independent companies repeatedly share counterparties, agents, directors, or payment behavior) and **pass-through laundering** (entities with little operating substance move high-value third-party money without a credible business purpose).
- **Evidence & sources**: 
  - Leaked/obtained bank transaction records for Tormex, Sirena, and Nomirex (bank records obtained by reporters from confidential sources) ([main investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/the-proxy-platform)).
  - UK, New Zealand, Latvian, Moldovan, Panamanian, and other company-registration records (public or registry-request records assembled cross-border) ([main investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/the-proxy-platform)).
  - Moldovan litigation and law-enforcement records plus interviews and site checks (public/request-gated records and reporter verification) ([opening investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/opening-the-door-proxy-platform-revealed)).
- **Access tier**: **mixed** — bank ledgers were privileged; registries and court files were open-public or request-gated; the consolidated cross-border transaction/company graph was constructed.
- **Detection signature**: Bank-ledger counterparties for Tormex, Sirena, and Nomirex joined to corporate registries on company name, registration number, address, director, and formation agent revealed a dense group of high-throughput shells with shared infrastructure rather than three unrelated firms ([main investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/the-proxy-platform)).
- **Corroboration structure**: Reporters re-anchored payment rows to legal entities in national registries, tested nominal directors and addresses through interviews/site reporting, and connected transactions to court and law-enforcement matters; no single leaked ledger was treated as proof of the underlying crime ([opening investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/opening-the-door-proxy-platform-revealed); [main investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/the-proxy-platform)).
- **Methodology notes**: **OCCRP-described**: reporters started from records for three companies, identified repeated counterparties and formation agents, and expanded jurisdiction by jurisdiction ([main investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/the-proxy-platform)). **[Inferred boundary]** High transaction volume or a shared agent alone is not laundering; the signature becomes probative when pass-through behavior, false or capacity-mismatched directors, no operating footprint, and crime-linked flows converge. **Official impact:** Russia’s central-bank chair later said one organized group accounted for over half of Russia’s $49 billion in questionable 2012 outflows; OCCRP linked that description to the network it had profiled ([OCCRP impact report](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/russias-central-bank-chair-on-the-proxy-platform)).
- **Generalization**: Apply to correspondent-banking data, procurement vendors, grant intermediaries, marketplace sellers, or political committees: build a bipartite entity-counterparty graph, enrich it with shared officers/addresses/agents, and rank low-substance entities by throughput, counterparty overlap, and role mismatch.

### SUB-ENTRY — A bad-tire lawsuit opened the platform (2011) — A small Moldovan civil dispute became the seed for a global shell network
- **URL**: [“Opening the Door: Proxy Platform Revealed”](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/opening-the-door-proxy-platform-revealed).
- **Partner/awards** (member centers/lead outlets; prizes): OCCRP and RISE Moldova supplied the court-to-company reporting inside the wider OCCRP consortium; award attribution remains at project level ([project team](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/about-us); [awards](https://www.occrp.org/en/about-us/awards)).
- **What they found** (2-4 concrete bullets — names, amounts, scale):
  - A Moldovan dispute over a **$437,176** defective-tire transaction identified Tormex and exposed payment and ownership links to Sirena and Nomirex ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/opening-the-door-proxy-platform-revealed)).
  - The dispute’s apparently ordinary commercial paperwork provided names, counterparties, and accounts that could be pivoted into registries and bank records ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/opening-the-door-proxy-platform-revealed)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **litigation-seed expansion** (a bounded court dispute supplies stable identifiers that unlock a much larger hidden network).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - Moldovan civil-court pleadings, exhibits, and decisions (public/request-gated judicial records) ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/opening-the-door-proxy-platform-revealed)).
  - Bank records and cross-border company files reached from names and accounts in the case (privileged bank data plus public/request-gated registries) ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/opening-the-door-proxy-platform-revealed)).
- **Access tier**: **mixed** — the seed case was open-public/request-gated; transaction records were privileged; the expanded entity graph was constructed.
- **Detection signature**: Court exhibits joined to bank ledgers and registries on company name, account, director, and address revealed that a one-off commercial plaintiff/defendant sat inside a repeated multi-company payment platform ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/opening-the-door-proxy-platform-revealed)).
- **Corroboration structure**: Court documents established that the entities and transaction actually existed; registries established legal identities; independent bank rows showed that the relationship recurred well beyond the pleaded tire deal ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/opening-the-door-proxy-platform-revealed)).
- **Methodology notes**: **[Inferred from the story’s reconstruction]** Treat litigation exhibits as identifier-rich discovery documents, not merely narratives: extract every entity, account, address, signatory, invoice, and intermediary before assessing the merits. **[Inferred boundary]** A litigated transaction may be genuine; network expansion must show repeated external relationships rather than assuming the lawsuit is a laundering pretext. **Official impact:** the project-level network was later identified by Russia’s central-bank chair as a major organized questionable-flow channel ([impact report](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/russias-central-bank-chair-on-the-proxy-platform)).
- **Generalization**: A generic detector mines court exhibits and judgments for stable identifiers, joins them to corporate and transaction datasets, then ranks cases whose parties connect to unusually dense or high-throughput networks.

### SUB-ENTRY — Magnitsky proceeds met a dormant UK company (2011) — Transfer records linked stolen-tax proceeds to Nomirex despite its registry status
- **URL**: [“Following the Magnitsky Money”](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/following-the-magnitsky-money).
- **Partner/awards** (member centers/lead outlets; prizes): Novaya Gazeta and OCCRP reporting within the Proxy Platform consortium; project was a 2013 Daniel Pearl Award finalist ([project team](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/about-us); [awards](https://www.occrp.org/en/about-us/awards)).
- **What they found** (2-4 concrete bullets — names, amounts, scale):
  - Bunicon and Elenast received about **$52 million** from Krainiy Sever in a short February 2008 window tied to the **$230 million** Russian tax fraud exposed by Sergei Magnitsky ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/following-the-magnitsky-money)).
  - Nomirex appeared in the flow even though its UK filings portrayed it as inactive, exposing a gap between legal-form status and financial behavior ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/following-the-magnitsky-money)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **proceeds re-entry** (traceable suspected-crime proceeds enter a broader payment network) and **registry-status mismatch** (an entity’s filed status or business capacity conflicts with observed financial activity).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - Bank-transfer records mapping the Magnitsky-related flow (privileged bank records) ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/following-the-magnitsky-money)).
  - UK Companies House filings and Russian case material (open-public/request-gated registry and judicial records) ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/following-the-magnitsky-money)).
- **Access tier**: **mixed** — privileged transaction data joined to open-public/request-gated corporate and case records; the flow reconstruction was constructed.
- **Detection signature**: A known-proceeds transaction chain joined to UK registry status on company name and date revealed that a company represented as inactive was receiving or transmitting crime-linked funds ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/following-the-magnitsky-money)).
- **Corroboration structure**: The criminal-case amount and source entities anchored the first leg; bank rows established timing and counterparties; Companies House filings independently tested whether the recipient’s declared activity could explain the flow ([story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-proxy-platform/following-the-magnitsky-money)).
- **Methodology notes**: **[Inferred from OCCRP’s flow account]** Use a confirmed or formally alleged proceeds source as a graph seed, but label downstream entities by degree of connection rather than imputing the seed offense to every recipient. **[Inferred boundary]** Dormant-company filings can be late or inaccurate and do not alone prove laundering. **Official impact:** the broader platform was publicly linked by Russia’s central-bank chair to a dominant questionable-flow group ([impact report](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/russias-central-bank-chair-on-the-proxy-platform)).
- **Generalization**: Compare transaction dates and values with registry lifecycle, tax-exempt status, license status, or declared operating purpose; flag entities transacting materially outside their legally reported capacity.

### PROJECT — The Russian Laundromat (2014/2017) — Fake debts and Moldovan judgments routed $20.8 billion into the global banking system
- **URL**: [2014 project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat); [2017 bank-record expansion](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed); [2017 findings](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-russian-laundromat-exposed).
- **Partner/awards** (member centers/lead outlets; prizes): OCCRP and Novaya Gazeta led the initial work; RISE Moldova, the Guardian, Süddeutsche Zeitung, and many national partners participated in the 2017 expansion through OCCRP’s secure collaboration ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)). The investigation was shortlisted in European Press Prize categories when OCCRP received a special award ([award report](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/occrp-wins-special-award-at-the-european-press-prize)).
- **What they found** (2-4 concrete bullets — names, amounts, scale):
  - From 2010 to early 2014, fake loan guarantees and more than 50 Moldovan court orders involving over 20 judges gave a lawful-looking basis for moving Russian funds through Moldindconbank and Latvia’s Trasta Komercbanka ([2014 investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat/the-russian-laundromat)).
  - The 2017 reconstruction found **$20.8 billion**, 21 core shell companies, **26,746 payments**, and beneficiaries in **96 countries**; the underlying datasets contained roughly 75,000 transfers ([2017 findings](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-russian-laundromat-exposed); [methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **judicial legitimation** (a sham or collusive legal process manufactures an enforceable pretext for moving money) and **legal-pretext laundering** (contracts, invoices, or judgments explain transfers whose underlying commerce is unsupported or fabricated).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - Two confidential bank datasets from Moldindconbank and Trasta Komercbanka, plus PDFs and records from multiple sources (privileged bank records) ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)).
  - Moldovan court orders, loan/guarantee instruments, invoices, and criminal-investigation records (open-public/request-gated judicial and law-enforcement material) ([2014 investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat/the-russian-laundromat)).
  - National and international registries, sanctions lists, and bank KYC records (open-public/request-gated sources plus privileged compliance files) ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project); [banks story](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-banks)).
- **Access tier**: **mixed** — bank/KYC data was privileged; court, registry, and sanctions data was open-public or request-gated; OCCRP’s normalized transaction database and cross-border graph were constructed.
- **Detection signature**: Moldovan judgments and guarantees joined to bank transfers on debtor, creditor, guarantor, account, amount, and date revealed a repeated machine in which Russian companies “defaulted,” Moldovan courts validated debt, and banks routed the resulting payments abroad ([2014 investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat/the-russian-laundromat); [methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)).
- **Corroboration structure**: OCCRP normalized two independent bank ledgers, matched companies against sanctions lists and registries, linked payment rows back to court orders and invoice sets, and used national partners to verify beneficiaries and local records; the legal documents established the payment pretext while the ledgers exposed its repeated system-level use ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)).
- **Methodology notes**: **OCCRP-described**: the team converted PDFs and multiple-source bank data into a reproducible database using about 1,500 lines of SQL/Python, screened company names against sanctions lists first, then used national registries and Investigative Dashboard for ownership verification ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)). **[Inferred boundary]** Court-enforced cross-border debt is ordinary commerce unless repeated templates, shared shells/proxies, unsupported invoices, and pass-through timing demonstrate coordination. **Official impact:** agencies in the UK, Switzerland, Moldova, and elsewhere announced inquiries; Moldova investigated judges and bank officials, while Trasta’s license had been withdrawn for anti-money-laundering failures ([world response](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-russian-laundromat-the-world-responds)).
- **Generalization**: Search any adjudicated-payment system—civil judgments, arbitration awards, insurance claims, tax refunds, bankruptcy distributions—for repeat parties, copied instruments, improbable guarantors, and money that exits immediately after official validation.

### SUB-ENTRY — Moldovan courts manufactured the payment key (2014) — Repeated guarantees and judgments converted sham debt into bankable obligations
- **URL**: [“The Russian Laundromat”](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat/the-russian-laundromat).
- **Partner/awards** (member centers/lead outlets; prizes): OCCRP, Novaya Gazeta, and RISE Moldova supplied the central court and bank reconstruction; awards are recorded at project level ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project); [award report](https://www.occrp.org/en/news/occrp-wins-special-award-at-the-european-press-prize)).
- **What they found** (2-4 concrete bullets — names, amounts, scale):
  - More than **20 judges in 15 Moldovan courts** issued over 50 orders validating supposed debts backed by Moldovan guarantors ([2014 investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat/the-russian-laundromat)).
  - The mechanism involved more than 90 Russian companies and 19 Russian banks, converting nominal defaults into orders payable through Moldindconbank ([2014 investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat/the-russian-laundromat)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **institutionalized pretext repetition** (the same legal or administrative pathway repeatedly validates transactions for a connected network).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - Moldovan court orders and case files (open-public/request-gated judicial records) ([2014 investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat/the-russian-laundromat)).
  - Guarantee agreements and loan documents (court exhibits and confidential/source-provided records) ([2014 investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat/the-russian-laundromat)).
  - Bank transfers implementing the orders (privileged bank records) ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)).
- **Access tier**: **mixed** — judicial records were open-public/request-gated; some instruments and bank rows were privileged; the case-pattern table was constructed.
- **Detection signature**: Court cases compared across court, judge, debtor, creditor, guarantor, amount, and filing date revealed repeated structures; joining those cases to bank execution records showed that judgments were the common authorization layer for outward transfers ([2014 investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat/the-russian-laundromat)).
- **Corroboration structure**: Each legal order was checked against the named companies and accounts, then linked to the actual bank movement; the number and repetition of cases reduced reliance on any single disputed judgment ([2014 investigation](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat/the-russian-laundromat); [methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)).
- **Methodology notes**: **[Inferred from OCCRP’s case reconstruction]** Normalize litigation into one row per obligation and compare document templates as well as parties. **[Inferred boundary]** High-volume debt collection can create legitimate repetition; false-positive control requires evidence of non-operating shells, implausible guarantees, or unsupported underlying trade. **Official impact:** Moldovan authorities investigated 16 judges and sent 14 cases to court, while bank officials also faced investigation or prosecution ([world response](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-russian-laundromat-the-world-responds)).
- **Generalization**: A detector groups judgments or administrative orders by template similarity and shared actors, then measures how often funds leave the jurisdiction immediately after the order.

### SUB-ENTRY — The 2017 bank leak exposed the destination layer (2017) — Normalized transfers turned a mechanism into a 96-country beneficiary map
- **URL**: [“The Russian Laundromat Exposed”](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-russian-laundromat-exposed); [project methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project).
- **Partner/awards** (member centers/lead outlets; prizes): OCCRP and Novaya Gazeta coordinated a secure cross-border workspace with RISE Moldova, the Guardian, Süddeutsche Zeitung, and national partners who investigated local recipients ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)).
- **What they found** (2-4 concrete bullets — names, amounts, scale):
  - Twenty-one core shells made **26,746 payments** into 96 countries, including payments to major companies and Russian state contractors ([2017 findings](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-russian-laundromat-exposed)).
  - Repeated and apparently copied invoices supplied supposed commercial explanations for transfers from the same small shell core ([2017 findings](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-russian-laundromat-exposed)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **beneficiary fan-out** (a compact origin or layering network distributes funds to a much larger recipient universe) and **document-template anomaly** (repeated, copied, or semantically implausible supporting documents belie distinct claimed transactions).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - Moldindconbank and Trasta Komercbanka transaction exports plus source PDFs (privileged bank data) ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)).
  - Payment invoices and bank documentation (privileged/source-provided transactional records) ([2017 findings](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-russian-laundromat-exposed)).
  - Sanctions lists and national corporate registries researched by local partners (open-public/request-gated reference data) ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)).
- **Access tier**: **mixed** — privileged bank/invoice records; open-public/request-gated watchlists and registries; constructed normalized database.
- **Detection signature**: Two bank datasets normalized into one transfer schema and joined on sender/recipient account, company, amount, currency, and date revealed the 21-shell fan-out; invoice texts clustered by repeated wording and commodity descriptions exposed copied commercial pretexts ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project); [2017 findings](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-russian-laundromat-exposed)).
- **Corroboration structure**: Account-level overlap reconciled the two bank views; sanctions and registry matches prioritized recipients; national partners then checked beneficial ownership, business activity, and local records before publication ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)).
- **Methodology notes**: **OCCRP-described**: reproducible parsing and a shared database let reporters query roughly 75,000 transfers, after which local partners supplied jurisdictional context ([methodology](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/about-the-project)). **[Inferred boundary]** A recipient’s appearance proves receipt, not knowledge of laundering; recipient culpability requires separate evidence about goods, services, ownership, or notice. **Official impact:** regulators and investigators in multiple recipient jurisdictions opened reviews after publication ([world response](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-russian-laundromat-exposed/the-russian-laundromat-the-world-responds)).
- **Generalization**: Normalize heterogeneous payment exports, identify a compact set of high-centrality origin nodes, cluster memo/invoice text, and distribute recipient research to reporters or analysts with local registry access.

### PROJECT — The Azerbaijani Laundromat (2017) — Four UK shells moved $2.9 billion while funding elite consumption and influence operations
- **URL**: [OCCRP project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-azerbaijani-laundromat); [how the system worked](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-azerbaijani-laundromat/what-is-a-laundromat); [raw-data note](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-azerbaijani-laundromat/the-raw-data).
- **Partner/awards** (member centers/lead outlets; prizes): Berlingske obtained the banking data and shared it with OCCRP, which organized reporting with the Guardian, Süddeutsche Zeitung, Le Monde, Czech Center for Investigative Journalism, Bulgarian Investigative Journalism Center, and other partners; the work also sat within the OCCRP/Transparency International Global Anti-Corruption Consortium ([project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-azerbaijani-laundromat)).
- **What they found** (2-4 concrete bullets — names, amounts, scale):
  - Nearly 17,000 payments moved about **$2.9 billion** through four UK-registered companies’ accounts at Danske Bank’s Estonian branch between 2012 and 2014 ([project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-azerbaijani-laundromat); [raw-data note](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-azerbaijani-laundromat/the-raw-data)).
  - The system mixed state-linked inputs, including funds tied to the International Bank of Azerbaijan and ministries, with payments benefiting officials, politicians, a journalist, luxury purchases, and other recipients ([project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-azerbaijani-laundromat)).
- **Finding type(s)**: **state-origin layering** (public or state-linked funds enter opaque private payment chains) and **influence laundering** (a laundering vehicle also pays intermediaries whose public actions benefit the originating regime or interest).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - Leaked Danske Estonia payment ledger covering four UK companies (privileged bank data obtained by Berlingske and shared with OCCRP) ([project](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-azerbaijani-laundromat); [raw-data note](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/the-azerbaijani-laundromat/the-raw-data)).

| Interviews, right-of-reply, site reporting, or recipient confirmation | 14 | Test identity, purpose, capacity, beneficial control, and alternative explanations. |
| Sanctions, PEP, adverse-event, or public-role reference data | 13 | Prioritize public-interest matches and create time-aware risk screens. |
| Property, land, rental, or other asset records | 8 | Trace conversion into durable assets and verify current or historical ownership. |
| FOI, customs, border, flight, licensing, or administrative-request records | 7 | Expand leak coverage, validate movement, and create denominators. |

The central sourcing lesson is triangular: a privileged leak supplies the hidden edge; a registry/court/official record supplies legal identity and status; a third source—another ledger, document, interview, asset record, or local partner—tests whether the edge means what it appears to mean. Three outlets holding the same leak would be redundancy, not corroboration.

### 2. Recurring detection signatures

These are this report’s own analytic codes. Coding records a signature only when it is a primary or major move in the entry, rather than every incidental lookup; an entry can have more than one signature.

| Detection-signature code | Entries | Precise recurring move |
|---|---:|---|
| **identity-anchor join** | 22 | Join leak rows to people/entities with account, passport, DOB, registration number, address, or another stable identifier. |
| **cross-silo graph expansion** | 20 | Pivot from one case/entity into registries, cases, assets, communications, and counterparties to reveal a larger network. |
| **flow-chain reconstruction** | 18 | Order transfers or value events into source → layering → beneficiary/asset paths. |
| **time-window alignment** | 16 | Compare payments or document state with sanctions, court orders, public actions, purchases, or other dated events. |
| **paper-versus-performance test** | 13 | Compare a contract, invoice, judgment, loan, or declared purpose with actual asset/service movement and recipient capacity. |
| **shared-infrastructure clustering** | 10 | Group nominally separate entities by agents, directors, addresses, emails, accounts, or synchronized counterparties. |
| **role/capacity mismatch** | 9 | Compare transaction/asset scale with declared business, filed status, occupation, license, or public income. |
| **snapshot/status diff** | 6 | Compare two leaks or a leak and current registry to detect acquisition, retention, transfer, closure, or changed legal status. |

The most characteristic OCCRP move is not “search the leak for famous names.” It is **stable-identifier resolution followed by a cross-silo join and a time-aware flow reconstruction**. The best stories then add a paper-versus-performance test: did the court order, invoice, loan, consultancy, share deal, or stated compliance date correspond to what actually happened?

### 3. Transferable pattern candidates

#### Pattern candidate A — The Pass-Through Constellation

- **Mechanics**: Build an account/entity flow graph; identify low-retention nodes; pair inflows and outflows within a short window; cluster nodes by shared officers, formation agents, addresses, accounts, invoices, or recipients. The pattern generalizes Proxy, Russian, Azerbaijani, and Troika mechanics.
- **Minimum data**: Transaction date, amount, currency, sender/recipient identifier, account or entity ID; at least one corporate-registry source; preferably declared business activity and balances.
- **Recognition cues**: Near-zero retained balances, same-day equal/near-equal values, rotating memo text, compact shell core with wide beneficiary fan-out, shared agents/proxies, and throughput far above operational capacity.
- **Boundary/falsification**: Payment processors, correspondent banks, licensed remitters, and corporate cash pools are legitimate pass-throughs. Falsify the laundering hypothesis with licenses, audited client-funds treatment, real services, arm’s-length ownership, and reconciled settlement records.

#### Pattern candidate B — Paper Truth / Economic Truth

- **Mechanics**: Model a legal document as a promised event—loan, sale, judgment, service, asset transfer, cancellation—and join it to the observed cash, asset, delivery, and ownership events. Russian court orders, copied invoices, Roldugin’s canceled share deals, the Riviera loan loop, and Abramovich’s outside-club payments instantiate the pattern.
- **Minimum data**: Contracts/invoices/orders with parties, dates, amounts, and purpose; payment or asset records; registry or delivery evidence sufficient to test performance.
- **Recognition cues**: Copied wording, serial cancellations, missing asset movement, circular repayment, implausible goods/services, off-books payors, non-market fees, and the same officials or guarantors across nominally distinct cases.
- **Boundary/falsification**: Legitimate commerce produces failed deals, templates, and related-party loans. Falsify with delivery records, market pricing, independent counterparties, tax/accounting recognition, genuine disputes, and asset-title movement.

#### Pattern candidate C — The Control Plane Behind the Shells

- **Mechanics**: Separate legal ownership from operational control. Join formation-agent records, fee payments, email domains/headers, document authors, KYC instructions, signatories, and shared addresses to the entity/transaction graph. Proxy’s formation agents, Troika/IOS, Cypriot service providers, and Riviera’s front executives are variants.
- **Minimum data**: Corporate registrations plus at least one operational source—email, invoice, KYC file, instruction, service payment, signature, device, or document metadata.
- **Recognition cues**: One service provider creates many transacting nodes; client documents originate from a bank/parent domain; nominal directors cannot explain activity; the same staff issue instructions across companies; service fees recur from network accounts.
- **Boundary/falsification**: Professional agents legitimately administer many companies. Falsify common control when instructions originate with independent clients, services are commodity/arm’s-length, directors demonstrate informed control, and transaction behavior is not network-coordinated.

#### Pattern candidate D — The Effective-Time Trap

- **Mechanics**: Convert sanctions, charges, court orders, regulatory deadlines, public actions, and account/asset events into intervals or state transitions; compare them with email, approval, signature, settlement, registry, and payment timestamps. Suisse Secrets’ relationship overlap, Azerbaijani influence timing, Cyprus’s TUI transfer, and Dubai sales are variants.
- **Minimum data**: A legally or analytically meaningful event timestamp; transaction/relationship open-close or document-state timestamps; resolved party/asset identity.
- **Recognition cues**: Urgent restructuring near a cutoff, approvals after claimed completion, accounts retained after public risk events, transfers immediately before action, or asset sales after charge/sanction.
- **Boundary/falsification**: Timing alone does not prove knowledge, intent, or legal completion. Falsify with evidence the operative act preceded the cutoff, the institution lacked notice, later emails were ministerial, or the transaction was independently required and fully disclosed.

#### Pattern candidate E — The Leak-to-Asset Exit

- **Mechanics**: Seed from a known flow, case graph, contractor list, or high-risk-person list; resolve owners in property/asset data; compare acquisition, financing, rental, transfer, and current-title events. Plunder, Riviera, Suisse case seeding, and Dubai Unlocked demonstrate increasing levels of this method.
- **Minimum data**: Resolved person/company graph; property or asset ownership with dates and identifiers; source-flow, public-contract, case, sanctions, or wealth data; ideally financing and current title.
- **Recognition cues**: Purchase soon after suspicious inflow, asset held through a new/related company, loan-sale round trips, wealth inconsistent with visible income, post-charge disposal, and clusters of units generating material rent.
- **Boundary/falsification**: Association with a case or public contract does not establish illicit source of funds. Falsify with a documented continuous lawful funding chain, pre-existing wealth, arm’s-length financing, divestment before the risk event, or an exculpatory/terminated official investigation.

### 4. What this platform can run today

**Runnable today with the stated local stack**

- **Control-plane and shell-convergence screens**: OpenAleph can index emails, contracts, invoices, and case files; UK Companies House plus 20+ registries can expose shared officers, addresses, agents, lifecycle conflicts, and formation bursts; the ICIJ Offshore Leaks Database can add offshore entities and intermediaries.
- **Identity-anchor and cross-leak pivots**: OpenAleph plus the Offshore Leaks data can connect names, companies, addresses, and source-document identifiers. GLEIF can normalize legal entities and LEIs; registry records can confirm legal status and jurisdiction.
- **Risk-event overlap**: OpenSanctions supplies sanctions/PEP/adverse-event entities and dates. Joined to locally held relationship, company, contract, or asset dates, it can run the Effective-Time Trap and prioritize high-risk relationships.
- **Case-graph-to-company screens**: Indictments, judgments, government releases, and audits ingested into OpenAleph can seed entities, which can then be joined to GLEIF, OpenSanctions, Companies House, other registries, and Offshore Leaks.
- **Paper-versus-performance on public records**: Where contracts, court orders, invoices, corporate filings, and asset-title events are already local or publicly retrievable, the platform can cluster templates and test promised events against observable corporate or ownership changes.

**Missing or materially incomplete**

- **Transaction substrate**: The named laundromat signatures require row-level bank/SWIFT ledgers with accounts, counterparties, dates, amounts, currencies, memos, and correspondent routes. The current source list does not itself supply that privileged substrate.
- **Account/KYC and execution state**: Sparse account-holder lists cannot reveal pass-through velocity, balances, bank knowledge, signatories, or when a transfer legally settled. KYC files, internal emails, approvals, and payment instructions remain leak/insider dependent.
- **Comprehensive dated asset data**: Dubai-grade property snapshots, current land-registry access, sale prices, financing, beneficial ownership, and utilities are not available globally. Public registries alone cannot run a reliable Leak-to-Asset Exit detector.
- **Normalized court, customs, border, procurement, and FOI event tables across jurisdictions**: OpenAleph can hold the documents, but the joins need deliberate extraction of case IDs, persons, dates, amounts, vessels, awards, travel, and outcomes.
- **Exposure denominators and negative evidence**: NarcoFiles-style rate claims need complete-enough administrative datasets, coverage metadata, deduplication rules, and enforcement-intensity measures. Search hits are not a denominator.
- **Human/local verification**: Transliteration, nominee relationships, document law, property status, and service-provider practice still require jurisdictional reporters or researchers. Automated matches should remain leads until a hard identifier and primary record support publication.

The immediate methodological opportunity is therefore **not** to imitate a bank leak without bank data. It is to precompute the reusable public side of OCCRP’s joins: canonical entities, aliases, officers, addresses, formation agents, sanctions/PEP intervals, court-case seeds, and known assets. When a privileged ledger or document corpus arrives, those anchors turn months of first-pass resolution into targeted verification.
