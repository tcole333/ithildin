### HUD's House of Cards (2018–2019, updated 2022) — HUD's own inspection system passes housing that is dangerous to live in
- **URL**: series https://www.propublica.org/series/huds-house-of-cards; flagship: https://www.propublica.org/article/hud-inspections-pass-dangerous-apartments-with-rats-roaches-toxic-mold (Nov 16, 2018); companion: https://www.propublica.org/article/hud-inspection-system-fails-low-income-tenants; news app: https://projects.propublica.org/hud
- **Partner/awards**: ProPublica Local Reporting Network partnership with The Southern Illinoisan (reporter Molly Parker); later items with The Republican (Springfield, MA), The Capital (Annapolis), Lee Enterprises Midwest. HUD Inspect app built with ProPublica news apps team (Dan Nguyen, Sophie Chou).
- **What they found**:
  - HUD's REAC (Real Estate Assessment Center) inspections repeatedly passed properties with severe hazards: Clay Arsenal Renaissance (Hartford, CT) scored 74 (2016) and 73 (2017) despite documented rats and mold; an Aug 2017 inspection of a Richmond, VA public housing project estimated ~820 health-and-safety violations yet the property passed.
  - National failure rates roughly tripled after HUD tightened protocols in 2016 (public housing 4% -> 13%, 2015->2017; private project-based 2% -> 5%), revealing how lenient the prior baseline was; meanwhile HUD's private multifamily inspection volume fell from ~8,400 (2015) to ~4,900 (2017).
  - A consultant described gaming inspections by literally walling off problem areas before inspectors arrived ("How a Consultant Said He Gamed HUD Inspections," Dec 21, 2018, https://www.propublica.org/article/how-a-consultant-said-he-gamed-hud-inspections-sweep-problems-behind-a-wall); HUD decertified 50+ contract inspectors in 2016–2017.
  - Internal documents showed HUD delayed acting on the collapsing Cairo, IL complexes for fear of political fallout (https://www.propublica.org/article/afraid-of-political-repercussions-hud-delayed-action-on-crumbling-public-housing); NYC properties passed despite HUD's own tally of numerous violations (https://www.propublica.org/article/hud-tallied-numerous-violations-in-new-york-city-public-housing).
- **Finding type(s)**: *oversight-theater* (an official scoring/certification regime systematically passes failing subjects, so the score becomes cover rather than control); *gamed-metric* (regulated parties optimize the inspection artifact, not the condition it proxies); *politicized-enforcement-delay* (agency withholds action for reputational/political reasons, shown by internal records).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - REAC inspection scores/reports — federal inspection database obtained via FOIA/records requests by the Southern Illinoisan; became the HUD Inspect lookup app.
  - City-level occupancy/code inspection reports (e.g., Hartford, Feb 2018) — municipal records, used as an independent ground-truth reading of the same buildings.
  - HUD enforcement paper trail — notices of default, contract terminations, inspector decertifications.
  - Internal HUD documents showing political delay — obtained through records requests/leaks [method not fully stated; inferred from article framing].
  - Tenant testimony and site documentation — interviews, photographs, a Cornell rodent expert's report; congressional testimony (HUD IG) and GAO audit (Mar 2019) as independent corroboration.
  - Crowdsourced callout to residents nationwide ("What do you know about public housing?", Aug 8, 2018).
- **Detection signature**: *score-vs-ground-truth diff* — the federal REAC pass/fail score for a property was compared against independent observations of the same property at the same time (municipal inspection findings, the inspection report's own violation counts, tenant-documented conditions, later IG/GAO findings). Passing scores joined on property ID to contemporaneous evidence of hazard = the finding. A second, supporting move: *policy-shock inflection read* — when HUD tightened scoring in 2016, failure rates tripled; the discontinuity itself was used as evidence that the old scores were inflated.
- **Corroboration structure**: layered federal score -> municipal inspection -> on-site documentation -> insider accounts (consultant, decertified inspectors, former HUD officials) -> official watchdogs (HUD OIG, GAO) arriving at the same conclusion after publication.
- **Methodology notes**: No standalone "How we did this" page found; the HUD Inspect app page (https://projects.propublica.org/hud) documents the underlying REAC score data. Analytic approach [inferred] from article text: acquisition of the national REAC score file plus case-level reconstruction of inspection histories.
- **Official impact**: HUD Secretary Carson announced a wholesale re-examination of inspection procedures (Oct 2018); GAO issued a critical report (Mar 2019); congressional letters/hearings; HUD later replaced REAC with the NSPIRE inspection standard in 2023 [subsequent development; causal contribution inferred, not claimed by ProPublica in the fetched pages].
- **Generalization**: any regime where a regulator scores the entities it also funds — nursing-home star ratings, restaurant grades, bank exams, defense contractor quality audits. Generic detector: join official pass/fail scores to any independent contemporaneous measurement of the same subject (complaints, municipal citations, lawsuits, sensor data); flag high scores with high independent-severity, and watch for step-changes in failure rates when scoring rules change (reveals prior inflation).

### The Rent Racket (2015–2017) — NYC landlords took rent-stabilization tax breaks while ignoring the rent limits attached to them
- **URL**: series https://www.propublica.org/series/the-rent-racket; flagship: https://www.propublica.org/article/nyc-landlords-flout-rent-limits-but-still-rake-in-lucrative-tax-breaks (Nov 4, 2015); quantified core: https://www.propublica.org/article/landlords-fail-to-list-fifty-thousand-nyc-apartments-for-rent-limits (Nov 5, 2015); preferential-rent interactive (June 22, 2017, with Ken Schwencke and Hilary Fung).
- **Partner/awards**: ProPublica original (Cezary Podkul, Marcelo Rochabrun); one item with The Real Deal.
- **What they found**:
  - ~50,000 apartments in ~5,500 buildings (about 40% of ~15,000 properties receiving 421-a/J-51 tax breaks as of 2013) were never registered for rent stabilization as the tax breaks require, exposing tenants to unlimited increases while owners kept $100M+ in tax benefits; ~80% were repeat non-registrants (2009–2012).
  - Lease-level proof at The Driggs (Williamsburg): tenant quoted $2,875 but lease recorded a $3,400 "legal" rent; increases of 9% and 7% against allowed caps of 4% and 1%; building-wide, all 112 units used preferential rents with an average $686/unit gap between charged and registered rent — a mechanism for later jumps and accumulated overcharges. The building paid $47,000 in property tax instead of ~$678,000 (93% reduction).
  - Enforcement was structurally vacant: the Department of Finance grants the break but cannot revoke it for rent violations; HPD can revoke but doesn't police rents; the state's DHCR referred questions elsewhere; only 2 revocations in 3 years.
  - Statewide data later showed even more non-compliance (Jan 7, 2016), and the "preferential rent" loophole let landlords raise rents despite a rent freeze (Apr 25, 2017); the 1971 vacancy-decontrol vote was reconstructed as the origin story (Dec 15, 2016).
- **Finding type(s)**: *subsidy-compliance-gap* (a benefit is received while the legally attached obligation goes unmet — the core of the series); *enforcement-vacuum* (obligations exist on paper but no agency owns enforcement; jurisdictional seams engineered or tolerated); *registered-vs-charged divergence* (the official register and the tenant-facing reality differ per unit).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - NYC Department of Finance tax rolls — public listing of properties receiving 421-a and J-51 exemptions.
  - Rent-stabilization building lists/registration data (DHCR / Rent Guidelines Board master list of stabilized buildings) — public listings; unit-level DHCR registration data was FOIL-requested and denied, forcing building-level analysis and tenant-supplied records.
  - Tenant leases and DHCR rent-history printouts — supplied directly by tenants.
  - Housing-court case files — initial rent rolls for The Driggs surfaced in litigation papers.
  - Housing analysts (e.g., Community Service Society's Tom Waters; retired state analyst Stephen Werner, who noted registered-unit counts never matched housing-survey counts) — expert interpretation.
- **Detection signature**: *entitlement-roll JOIN obligation-register* — the roll of buildings receiving conditional tax benefits was cross-checked against the register of buildings/units actually enrolled in the obligation (rent stabilization). Set difference = violators, priced in dollars of tax expenditure. Stated methodology (paraphrased from the Nov 5, 2015 piece): ProPublica analyzed government data covering ~15,000 subsidized rental buildings and cross-checked stabilization registration against the 421-a/J-51 recipient list. A second signature at unit level: *lease-vs-registration diff* — tenant's actual lease terms vs the state-registered legal rent vs the legally allowed annual cap, yielding a per-unit overcharge estimate.
- **Corroboration structure**: dataset-level join (population count) + named-building case proof (leases, court rent rolls) + on-record confirmation from the Public Advocate's office after reviewing the same records + officials' acknowledgment of the registration gap.
- **Methodology notes**: methodology described inside the articles themselves (no separate page); the FOIL denial of unit-level data is explicitly reported — a documented data-access constraint that shaped the design [stated].
- **Official impact**: $5M settlement over rent-law dodging (Dec 3, 2015); NYC put 3,000 landlords on notice to comply or lose benefits (Nov 23, 2016); Cuomo's pledge to re-regulate 50,000 apartments audited by the series itself and found badly short (Dec 28, 2016); city bills followed, and the 2019 state rent-law overhaul later addressed preferential rents [2019 link is inferred context, not claimed in fetched pages].
- **Generalization**: identical structure wherever benefits carry strings — PPP loans with retention conditions, agricultural subsidies with conservation requirements, TIF districts with affordability covenants, nonprofit tax exemptions with charity-care obligations. Generic detector: build the recipient roll and the compliance register as separate tables, join on entity/parcel ID, and treat the anti-join as a priced violation population; then prove mechanism with a handful of unit-level document diffs.

### Checked Out (2023–2024) — LA let protected low-cost residential hotels become tourist hotels; reporters found the violators the city never looked for
- **URL**: series https://www.propublica.org/series/checked-out; flagship: https://www.propublica.org/article/how-la-failed-stop-landlords-turning-low-cost-housing-hotels (July 10, 2023); methodology piece: https://www.propublica.org/article/we-found-los-angeles-landlords-renting-residential-hotels (July 25, 2023); data sequel: https://www.propublica.org/article/los-angeles-housing-rent-controlled-apartments-vacation-rentals (July 31, 2024, with Haru Coryne).
- **Partner/awards**: co-reported by Capital & Main (Robin Urevich) and ProPublica (Gabriel Sandoval); photography Barbara Davidson.
- **What they found**:
  - 21 officially designated residential hotels (800+ rooms), protected as last-resort housing under LA's 2008 residential hotel ordinance, were openly advertising to tourists (up to $235/night), in apparent violation of city law; the city had essentially never enforced the ordinance.
  - Tenants of the American Hotel were displaced as rooms became tourist units; Housing Department correspondence confirmed each of the 21 buildings' protected status.
  - The 2024 sequel found 63 rent-controlled buildings bookable by tourists in spring 2023 and 100+ additional rent-controlled buildings registered with the LA Office of Finance as hotel businesses for tax purposes — the city's own tax arm registering what its housing arm prohibits; at 1940 Carmen Ave., 14 of 21 units were on Airbnb in 2020.
  - Follow-ups showed violators even received city homelessness-program contracts (Inside Safe, Nov 14, 2023: https://www.propublica.org/article/los-angeles-residential-hotels-got-inside-safe-contracts-despite-violations).
- **Finding type(s)**: *protected-inventory leakage* (units in a legally protected affordability category quietly re-enter the open market at tourist rates); *enforcement-vacuum*; *intra-government contradiction* (one city database licenses/taxes exactly the activity another city law bans — the government's own records disprove its enforcement posture).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - The city's official residential-hotel designation list (2008 and later) — public regulatory record; the protected baseline.
  - Commercial listing surfaces — hotel websites, Booking.com, Hotels.com, Airbnb, Vrbo, Yelp and Google reviews — open web, manually combed.
  - 10,000+ pages of Housing Department records — California Public Records Act requests (correspondence, hearing-officer decisions, inspector notes including a manager admitting transient operation).
  - LA Office of Finance hotel-business (transient occupancy tax) registration list — public/requested administrative data.
  - Field verification — site visits, photography of signage and tourists; historical Google Maps imagery to establish name changes.
- **Detection signature**: *protected-list JOIN commercial-listing* — the roster of legally protected residential buildings was matched against open-market booking listings (by name, address, and imagery when platforms mask addresses); any match is a violation candidate, then confirmed in official records. Stated methodology (paraphrased from the July 25, 2023 methods piece): reporters searched hotel webpages and travel sites, matched candidates to official residential designations, obtained 10K+ pages via CPRA, and visited properties; they ultimately identified 21 protected hotels with 800+ rooms marketed to tourists. The 2024 sequel adds a stronger variant: *cross-registry contradiction join* — rent-stabilization rolls joined to the city's own hotel-tax registrations, so the violation is proven entirely inside government data.
- **Corroboration structure**: baseline legal-status documents -> live commercial advertising -> physical observation -> the city's own inspector notes/correspondence acknowledging the same facts -> post-publication city investigations confirming.
- **Methodology notes**: explicit methodology article exists (July 25, 2023 URL above) [stated]. Limitation acknowledged in 2024 piece: platform address-masking makes the 63-building count a floor [stated].
- **Official impact**: LA Housing Department opened an investigation two days after publication (https://www.propublica.org/article/los-angeles-housing-department-to-investigate-residential-hotels, July 12, 2023); demand letters ordering hotels to stop renting to tourists (Aug 11 and Dec 21, 2023); mayor ordered residential hotels used for temporary homeless housing (Nov 4, 2023); proposed enforcement expansion (Oct 2, 2023) and a pre-Olympics crackdown debate (Dec 14, 2024).
- **Generalization**: any protected-inventory regime with open-market resale surfaces — rent-stabilized units on Airbnb, affordable-covenant condos listed at market rates, heritage-protected buildings marketed for conversion, export-controlled goods on trade platforms, diverted prescription drugs on gray markets. Generic detector: maintain the protected roster as a watchlist; continuously scrape the relevant marketplace; fuzzy-join on name/address/imagery; escalate matches to document confirmation. Where possible, prefer the government-vs-government variant: two official registries that cannot both be true.

### Swept Away (2024–2025) — Cities seize and destroy homeless people's essential belongings; storage programs are largely fictional
- **URL**: series https://www.propublica.org/series/swept-away; flagship interactive: https://projects.propublica.org/homeless-encampment-sweeps-taken-belongings (Oct 29, 2024); storage investigation: https://www.propublica.org/article/homeless-encampment-removals-property-storage (Nov 21, 2024); toll piece: https://projects.propublica.org/impact-of-homeless-sweeps-lost-belongings/ (Dec 27, 2024).
- **Partner/awards**: ProPublica original (Ruth Talbot, Asia Fields, Nicole Santa Cruz, Maya Miller).
- **What they found**:
  - Records from 14–16 cities documented what sweeps confiscate: 400+ logged references to tents, 400+ to sleeping bags/blankets, 1,300+ to clothing, 150+ to tools, 80+ to wheelchairs/walkers — plus birth certificates, medications, and cremated remains of loved ones.
  - Cities claim they store seized property for retrieval, but the records show storage is rare and retrieval rarer: San Diego removed 3,000+ encampments in 2023 but stored belongings 19 times; Seattle cleared ~1,000 encampments in six months and stored from 55; Portland's 12-month retrieval rate was 4%; San Francisco ~12% over 18 months; Anaheim 0% (Jan 2023–May 2024); Phoenix logged 69 storage instances against 4,900 encampment reports.
  - Of 95+ swept people ProPublica contacted, 30 tried to retrieve items; exactly 1 recovered everything; 65 didn't try because of transport, hours, or contact barriers.
  - Context: courts have held property destruction in sweeps can violate the 4th/14th Amendments; the June 2024 Grants Pass ruling enabled more aggressive removals (https://www.propublica.org/article/us-supreme-court-grants-pass-homelessness); Albuquerque was violating its own storage policy (https://www.propublica.org/article/albuquerque-homeless-encampments, May 16, 2024).
- **Finding type(s)**: *state-loss-of-property* (government seizure/destruction of the belongings of people with least recourse); *policy-vs-practice gap* (written policy promises storage; operational logs show near-zero delivery); *administrative-burden-as-denial* (retrieval formally possible but practically unreachable — the barrier is the mechanism).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - Sweep/storage logs and property inventories from 14 cities (16 surveyed for records) — standardized public-records requests; logs include item descriptions, brands, conditions, photos.
  - Retrieval statistics — city storage-program records obtained by records request.
  - 135 interviews with people who experienced sweeps, plus 200+ written accounts — direct outreach and a public callout (https://www.propublica.org/getinvolved/help-investigate-homeless-encampment-removals).
  - Court records from sweep lawsuits (LA, Phoenix, San Francisco, Denver, Albuquerque) — litigation files; DOJ's June 2024 Phoenix findings as federal corroboration.
  - Verification layer — sweep occurrence and timing checked against city schedules, data, media reports; identities checked via public records.
- **Detection signature**: *multi-jurisdiction records survey + policy-vs-practice diff* — the same records-request template fanned out across ~16 cities produced comparable operational logs; each city's stated storage policy was then diffed against its own storage/retrieval counts (sweeps performed vs storage events vs successful retrievals), converting a moral claim into a ratio. The interactive flagship adds *seized-item accumulation*: aggregating thousands of inventory-log line items into typed counts (survival gear, medical equipment, documents, sentimental items) so the object-level record itself becomes the finding. Stated verification methodology (paraphrased from the projects page): records of seized property obtained from 14 cities; displayed items selected as representative; every narrated sweep independently verified via interviews, city data, schedules or media reports, and identities via public records.
- **Corroboration structure**: city's own logs -> affected-person testimony (135) -> litigation/DOJ findings -> cross-city comparability (the pattern holds everywhere, so it is structural, not local mismanagement).
- **Methodology notes**: methodology stated on the project page itself (URL above) and in the responsible-engagement essay (https://www.propublica.org/article/reporting-on-homelessness-responsibly-guide-propublica, Feb 4, 2025) [stated].
- **Official impact**: cities responded with process defenses; San Francisco was court-ordered to improve worker training; DOJ had found Phoenix destroyed property without notice; no city directly defended the retrieval rates. (Series is recent; legislative outcomes not yet documented in fetched pages.)
- **Generalization**: any custody regime where the state takes property/records from low-power populations and claims a return process — civil asset forfeiture, jail property rooms, immigration detention belongings, towed-vehicle and storage-lien systems, evidence rooms. Generic detector: request (a) intake logs, (b) storage counts, (c) successful-return counts for the same period across many jurisdictions; the intake:return ratio is the story, and identical templates across jurisdictions turn anecdote into structure.

### Homeowner Hell (2022–2023) — Colorado HOAs foreclose on homes over small debts, and the process pays the lawyers regardless
- **URL**: series https://www.propublica.org/series/homeowner-hell; flagship: https://www.propublica.org/article/they-faced-foreclosure-not-from-their-mortgage-lender-but-from-their-hoa (Apr 7, 2022); law-firm piece: https://www.propublica.org/article/colorado-hoa-lawsuits-foreclosure-law-firms-paymah (Mar 11, 2023); reform check: "Are Colorado's Efforts to Curb HOA Foreclosures Working?" (Mar 11, 2023).
- **Partner/awards**: ProPublica Local Reporting Network partnership with Rocky Mountain PBS (Brittany Freeman); data analysis by Sophie Chou (ProPublica), research by Mariam Elba.
- **What they found**:
  - Colorado HOAs (governing ~2.6M residents, nearly half the state) filed 2,400+ foreclosure cases Jan 2018–Feb 2022; at least 215 ended in sheriff's sales stripping owners of their homes.
  - ~450 HOAs filed 730+ foreclosure cases during the pandemic (Apr 2020–Jul 2021) while bank/mortgage foreclosures were under state and federal moratoriums — HOAs used a carve-out the moratoria didn't cover.
  - Seven law firms each filed 100+ HOA foreclosures; Alcock Law Group filed 300+ since 2018; a typical uncontested case generates $4,000–$6,000 in attorney fees (per the firm's own court filing). Case study: Karl Paymah's $1,515.45 HOA debt grew to ~$5,000 in six weeks via fees and ultimately $25,774 to save the house; foreclosure threats began over dues as small as $308. One HOA (Rock Ridge) billed residents $76,000+ in legal charges.
  - The state cannot investigate HOA management companies even when ~$30K vanished from an HOA account (Aug 30, 2022) — a regulator-jurisdiction hole.
- **Finding type(s)**: *fee-engine capture* (an enforcement process whose economics reward the intermediaries — law firms — independent of outcomes, inflating tiny debts into home-loss events); *moratorium-carve-out exploitation* (activity surging through a legal pause via an uncovered actor class); *regulator-jurisdiction hole* (no agency empowered to investigate a whole actor category).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - Statewide court filing data — list of all Colorado foreclosure cases, from state court records; the HOA subset derived analytically (below).
  - Sheriff's-sale outcome records — court/county records linked to the filings.
  - Case files and a trial transcript (Paymah v. Rock Ridge) — court records.
  - HOA billing/financial records (Rock Ridge's $76K legal charges) — association documents.
  - A law firm's own court filing disclosing its typical fee range and ~50 HOA clients — litigation record used against interest.
  - Interviews: homeowners, HOA boards, HOA-side attorneys, legislators, advocates.
- **Detection signature**: *plaintiff-name keyword harvest of dockets* — stated methodology (paraphrased from the Apr 7, 2022 piece): HOA-initiated cases were isolated from the statewide foreclosure docket by searching plaintiff names for HOA-signature keywords, then manually reviewing every hit to eliminate false positives. On top of that base table, three derived moves: (a) *moratorium-window filter* — restricting the filing table to the COVID moratorium window shows the HOA carve-out surge; (b) *outcome chain-link* — joining filings to sheriff's-sale records converts "cases filed" into "homes actually lost" (215); (c) *fee-vs-principal ratio* — comparing attorney fees to underlying debt sizes exposes the process as the profit center.
- **Corroboration structure**: docket-derived population counts -> named-case document reconstruction -> the fee structure confirmed by the law firm's own filings -> adversarial interviews with HOA attorneys -> legislative testimony echoing the data.
- **Methodology notes**: methodology stated inline in the flagship (keyword search + manual review) [stated]; no separate methodology page found.
- **Official impact**: Colorado's HOA foreclosure reform bill (2022, HB22-1137) passed May 2022 — limits foreclosure paths, requires longer payment plans; proposed attorney-fee caps were dropped after industry negotiation; the Mar 2023 reform-check story audited the law's early effects.
- **Generalization**: any small-debt-to-large-forfeiture pipeline run through courts by a specialized bar — tax-lien foreclosures, condo/co-op liens, municipal-fine foreclosures, medical-debt garnishment mills, storage-lien auctions. Generic detector: pull the full docket for a case type; classify plaintiffs by name-pattern (association/servicer/firm signatures); rank law firms by filing volume; compute fee-to-principal ratios and outcome chains; and always re-run the filter inside any legal pause window to catch carve-out exploitation.

### When Private Equity Becomes Your Landlord (2022) — PE-backed firms became co-owners of the U.S. apartment market with government-backed financing
- **URL**: https://www.propublica.org/article/when-private-equity-becomes-your-landlord (Feb 7, 2022, Heather Vogell; series: Rent Barons)
- **Partner/awards**: ProPublica original.
- **What they found**:
  - By 2021, private-equity-backed firms held roughly 1 million of the units among the National Multifamily Housing Council's top-35 owners — about half, up from about a third in 2011; the top-50 owners' total holdings grew to 2.3M units (2M in 2017).
  - Freddie Mac — a government-sponsored enterprise chartered to support affordable housing — financed the consolidation: PE firms appeared in 85% of Freddie's 20 largest single-borrower apartment deals (five firms — Greystar, Lone Star, Starwood, Brookfield, Harbor Group — took half, borrowing ~$16B); Greystar's 2017 Olume-era acquisition financing (~$1.8B) was Freddie's largest deal to date.
  - Case-level conduct after acquisition: at the Olume (San Francisco), rents rose from $2,800 to $3,400+ within ~18 months, services/security declined, and building profit rose $2.33M (2018–2019, per Freddie data); pet rent rose 25%.
  - Freddie/FHFA tenant protections (30-day rent-increase notice for manufactured-home pad tenants, eviction-notice rules) arrived only in 2021 — after the consolidation. Manufactured-housing communities are flagged as the sector's next expansion target.
- **Finding type(s)**: *concentration shift with conduct change* (ownership-structure migration measurably changes tenant-facing behavior); *public-finance subsidy to extraction* (a public-mission lender bankrolling the acquirers driving rent escalation); *opacity-by-structure* (LLC/fund layering obscuring who the landlord is).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - NMHC annual top-owner rankings (2011–2021) — industry association data, longitudinal.
  - Funding-provenance classification — PitchBook, Crunchbase, Private Equity Real Estate, trade press, and firms' own sites used to label each top owner as PE-backed or not.
  - Freddie Mac deal data and Commercial Mortgage Alert — GSE and trade-publication records for the largest securitized apartment loans.
  - Building-level records — SF building-department complaints, police call logs, tenant leases/interviews (Olume before/after).
- **Detection signature**: *ownership-roster enrichment over time* — stated methodology (paraphrased from the article's methods note): the NMHC top-owner listing was taken as the population, each owner was classified by funding source using PitchBook/Crunchbase/PERE/trade-press/company materials, and the PE share was computed across years. The macro trend was then grounded with a *before/after acquisition diff* at a named building (rents, complaints, police calls, service levels pre- vs post-PE management) and a *lender-concentration slice* (share of the GSE's largest deals going to PE borrowers).
- **Corroboration structure**: industry roster (population) -> third-party financial databases (classification) -> GSE deal records (financing mechanism) -> building-level records and tenants (conduct) -> firms' responses.
- **Methodology notes**: classification methodology stated in the article [stated]; no separate page. Acquisition channel for the Freddie profit data not specified in the article.
- **Official impact**: FHFA/Freddie tenant-protection tweaks documented in-article (mostly announced 2021, pre-publication context); no direct enforcement outcome claimed. The story seeded the series that produced the RealPage investigation eight months later.
- **Generalization**: ownership-migration studies work anywhere a top-owner roster exists — hospitals/nursing homes (PE rollups), veterinary clinics, mobile-home parks, single-family rentals, newspapers, ambulance services. Generic detector: take an industry's periodic top-N owner table, enrich each owner with capital-source metadata (fund registries, SEC ADV filings, PitchBook), compute the backed-share trend, then prove conduct change with per-asset before/after records (prices, complaints, inspection/police calls) around ownership transitions — and check which public financing channel funded the shift.

### How Your Shadow Credit Score Could Decide Whether You Get an Apartment (2022) — Unregulated tenant-screening scores gatekeep housing with error-prone data
- **URL**: https://www.propublica.org/article/how-your-shadow-credit-score-could-decide-whether-you-get-an-apartment (Mar 29, 2022, Erin Smith and Heather Vogell; series: Rent Barons)
- **Partner/awards**: ProPublica original.
- **What they found**:
  - Nearly 2,000 companies sold background/tenant screening (~$1B/yr industry); RealPage's screening arm alone claimed a database of 30M lease outcomes; applicants are scored by opaque algorithms (RentGrow/Yardi, LeasingDesk/RealPage, On-Site, Credco) with no practical right to see or contest the score.

- **Evidence & sources**:

Combined with *repeat-denial case tracing* (following individual applicants through multiple applications to show the score, not the applicant, produced the outcomes) and *crowdsourced-harm registry* (callout survey to establish frequency beyond anecdote).
- **Corroboration structure**: individual documents -> survey pattern -> regulator correspondence proving the oversight hole -> the industry's own marketing claims (database sizes, predictive promises) used as admissions of scale.
- **Methodology notes**: no formal methodology section; survey and review described inline [stated but thin; analytic framing partly inferred].
- **Official impact**: none documented in the article beyond the pre-existing senators' letter; later CFPB attention to tenant screening is a subsequent development [inferred; not claimed in fetched text].
- **Generalization**: every consequential scoring system adjacent to a regulated one — employment background scores, insurance telematics, hospital admission-risk scores, gig-work deactivation algorithms. Generic detector: identify the regulated analog; enumerate its subject rights (access, dispute, adverse-action notice); test each against the shadow system via case documents and complaint-record pulls; the enumerated deltas are the finding.

### Housing Loophole Lets Wealthy Investors Raise Rents on Poor Tenants (2025) — The LIHTC "qualified contract" exit strips affordability 16 years early
- **URL**: https://www.propublica.org/article/affordable-housing-investors-loophole-rent-tenants (Feb 13, 2025, Jesse Coburn)
- **Partner/awards**: ProPublica original.
- **What they found**:
  - The "qualified contract" provision of the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit statute lets owners shed rent restrictions after ~14 years instead of the promised 30, via a buyout-price formula that almost always overvalues the property so no qualifying buyer appears — deregulation by designed-to-fail offer.   - Qualified-contract applications and state housing-agency filings — public-records requests to state agencies.
  - Property transaction records (purchase/sale prices, loan backing) — county records and public filings.
  - National Council of State Housing Agencies unit-loss estimates; Brookings/NLIHC shortage data — published research.
  - National Housing Trust policy tracking (which states closed the loophole) — advocacy-data interview.
  - The formula drafter on record — an architect's admission.
  - Company web/LinkedIn presence for entity-to-person resolution.
- **Detection signature**: *exit-mechanism tracking* — instead of studying the subsidy's entry side, follow the exit door: collect qualified-contract applications (the formal deregulation requests), join each to its property's subsequent sale price and rent trajectory, and aggregate to a national unit-loss count. - **Corroboration structure**: state filings (mechanism) -> transaction records (profit) -> tenant interviews (harm) -> national aggregate from NCSHA (scale) -> drafter's admission (design failure) -> industry defense on record.
- **Methodology notes**: no separate methodology page; sourcing largely stated inline (records requests, cited estimates) [stated]; join logic [inferred from article structure].
- **Official impact**: story documents that most states now require waiver of qualified-contract rights; congressional repeal failed most recently in 2023 against developer/PE lobbying; FHFA's 2023 direct-investment bar left the loan channel open; the White House did not respond to inquiries.
- **Generalization**: look for the exit clause in any time-bound public covenant — HUD mortgage prepayment exits, USDA rural-housing opt-outs, conservation-easement extinguishment, pension de-risking transfers, charter-school asset conversions. Generic detector: obtain the register of formal exit requests from a covenant regime; join to subsequent asset prices/rents; if a statutory formula gates the exit, model it against market values to test whether it is designed to fail.

### Contracts for Deed Put Somali Families at Risk (2022) — "Interest-free" seller financing sold to Muslim buyers at same-day markups with balloon traps
- **URL**: co-published: https://sahanjournal.com/housing/contracts-for-deed-put-somali-families-financial-risk-minnesota/ (Nov 21, 2022); ProPublica follow-ups: https://www.propublica.org/article/minnesota-attorney-general-investigation-contract-for-deed-real-estate; jury verdict: https://www.propublica.org/article/minnesota-jury-predatory-home-financing-chadwick-banken-contract-for-deed (June 23, 2026)
- **Partner/awards**: ProPublica (Jessica Lussenhop; data by Haru Coryne) with Sahan Journal (Joey Peters); later coverage with Andy Mannix.
- **What they found**:
  - Investors marketed contracts for deed as sharia-compliant "interest-free" homeownership to Minnesota's ~80,000-strong Somali community; sellers (notably Chad Banken's companies — 100+ properties sold since 2019, entities including Banken Holdings and "Slow Flip LLC") bought houses with conventional mortgages and resold them — sometimes the same day — at large markups with big down payments and five-year balloon payments reaching six figures.
  - At least 100 Somali families bought this way; 1,800+ contracts for deed were recorded in Minnesota's 11 most populous counties in 2021 alone (peak ~2,500 in 2012); interest was often present but concealed — front-loaded into inflated prices, contradicting the interest-free pitch.
  - Buyers risk total loss: under contract-for-deed default, occupants can be removed like tenants (eviction, not foreclosure, with its protections) and forfeit everything paid — one family stood to lose ~$100,000 already paid, with $500,000+ due at the balloon.
- **Finding type(s)**: *targeted-affinity predation* (a product engineered around a community's specific constraint — the religious prohibition on interest — used to overcharge that community); *designed-to-fail financing* (terms structured so default/churn, not repayment, is the profitable outcome); *deed-chain markup laundering* (the same-day A->B->C paper trail embedding the extraction in county records).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - County deed/property records across 11 counties — recorder data compiled to count contracts for deed and reconstruct purchase-resale chains and prices.
  - The contracts themselves — terms, balloons, rates, reviewed with buyers.
  - Historical federal court records — Banken's 2000s foreclosure-rescue litigation as pattern evidence.
  - Interviews: five-plus Somali buyer households, legal-aid attorneys, Islamic-finance providers, sellers on record (C4D LLC's Steven Legatt), community organizations.
  - Homeownership-gap statistics (Urban Institute, Wilder Foundation) — context for demand.
- **Detection signature**: *same-day deed-chain flip detection* — in county recorder data, find parcels with a conventional purchase by an investor followed immediately (same day or near) by a contract-for-deed resale of the same parcel at a materially higher price; the price delta measured across a seller's portfolio quantifies the markup practice. Layered with *seller-portfolio accumulation* (rolling up 100+ sales by one seller's related LLCs) and *contract-term autopsy* (balloon structures that cannot be refinanced; hidden effective interest vs the interest-free pitch).
- **Corroboration structure**: recorder-data pattern -> the paper contracts -> buyer testimony -> seller interviews/admissions -> historical litigation showing prior conduct -> later adversarial testing in court (2026 jury verdict for the state).
- **Methodology notes**: no formal methodology section; the 11-county counts imply systematic recorder-data compilation [stated counts; compilation method inferred].
- **Official impact**: Minnesota AG opened an investigation (Feb 2024) citing the reporting; reform legislation introduced and passed; the AG sued Banken; a June 2026 jury found the scheme violated state law (URLs above; also https://www.propublica.org/article/lawmakers-bill-reform-contract-for-deed-home-sales-minnesota).
- **Generalization**: affinity-targeted extraction recurs wherever a constraint blocks mainstream products — immigrant communities avoiding banks, unbanked buyers (buy-here-pay-here auto lots), religious lending niches, land-installment sales in disinvested cities (Colony Ridge below is the Texas cousin). Generic detector: in deed/title data, flag rapid same-parcel resales at high markup where the second instrument is seller-financed; cluster by seller-entity family; test marketing language against actual contract economics.

### Living Apart (2012, within series Segregation Now) — For 40 years the government refused to enforce the Fair Housing Act it administers
- **URL**: https://www.propublica.org/article/living-apart-how-the-government-betrayed-a-landmark-civil-rights-law (Oct 2012, updated June 25, 2015); series listing now under https://www.propublica.org/series/living-apart (resolves with Segregation Now items); tester audit: https://www.propublica.org/article/blacks-latinos-likely-to-face-housing-bias-in-whitest-parts-of-westchester
- **Partner/awards**: ProPublica original (Nikole Hannah-Jones); Sidney Award (https://www.propublica.org/atpropublica/propublicas-nikole-hannah-jones-wins-sidney-award); Columbia Tobenkin Award.
- **What they found**:
  - Since 1974, HUD had withheld Community Development Block Grant money over fair-housing violations only twice, while continuing to fund communities under court findings of promoting segregation (e.g., New Orleans); the Fair Housing Act's "affirmatively furthering" mandate went unenforced by every administration after Nixon killed George Romney's Open Communities program (which had briefly withheld funds from Boston, Baltimore, Toledo) and pushed Romney out.
  - Grantee compliance paperwork ("analyses of impediments") was routinely missing, outdated, or empty: a GAO review of 441 grantees found a third outdated and 25 with no analysis at all; a 2009 internal HUD study of 70 applicants showed similar failure; HUD conceded it did not check the paperwork and could not produce records of how often it had withheld grants.
  - Westchester County falsely certified compliance while taking millions — exposed by False Claims Act litigation, producing the rare enforcement settlement; paired-tester audits still found bias against Black and Latino homeseekers in Westchester's whitest areas (Feb 2013).
- **Finding type(s)**: *enforcement-abdication census* (an agency's entire enforcement history countable on one hand — the count is the finding); *certification-without-verification* (self-certified compliance never audited — a paperwork regime with no reader); *archival intent recovery* (private papers/memos establishing that non-enforcement was a deliberate political choice, not drift).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - Romney's private papers (Bentley Historical Library, U. Michigan) and Nixon-era memos (Ehrlichman/Haldeman) — archival research establishing the origin decision.
  - HUD enforcement records — FOIA; the agency's inability to produce withholding records became evidence itself.
  - GAO's 441-grantee file review and HUD's internal 70-applicant study — watchdog/internal audits repurposed as quantitative backbone.
  - Grantee "analyses of impediments" — the compliance documents themselves, reviewed for existence/currency/substance.
  - Interviews: Walter Mondale (the law's floor manager), former HUD assistant secretaries (Elizabeth Julian, Roberta Achtenberg) and career compliance staff.
  - Paired-tester audit results (Westchester) — fair-housing-group testing data.
- **Detection signature**: *enforcement-action census* — take the agency's full grant-making population over four decades and count the enforcement events against it; two withholdings against thousands of grantees quantifies abdication precisely. Paired with *dead-letter paperwork audit* (sampling the mandated compliance filings and scoring existence/currency/substance — the GAO 441-file review) and *records-absence-as-evidence* (the agency cannot say how often it enforced; the missing ledger is itself probative).
- **Corroboration structure**: archival intent (why it stopped) -> enforcement count (that it stopped) -> paperwork audit (how the void was papered over) -> insider testimony (that staff knew) -> live testing (that the harm persists) -> litigation case study (what enforcement looks like when forced).
- **Methodology notes**: the enforcement-count claim and HUD's records failure are stated explicitly in the article [stated]; archival sourcing described in text [stated].
- **Official impact**: the Obama administration's 2015 Affirmatively Furthering Fair Housing rule — requiring jurisdictions to document and combat segregation — followed the reporting arc (noted in the 2015 update); Westchester consent-decree enforcement continued.
- **Generalization**: works on any mandate with a self-certification loop — environmental self-reporting, bank CRA obligations, university Title IX/Clery compliance, contractor security attestations. Generic detector: (1) count actual enforcement events over the program's life vs the eligible population; (2) sample the mandated filings for existence and substance; (3) FOIA the enforcement ledger — treat inability to produce it as a finding; (4) look in leaders' archives for the moment enforcement was switched off.

### The Eviction Ban Worked (2020) — Docket-level proof that the CARES Act moratorium suppressed evictions, and where it didn't reach
- **URL**: https://www.propublica.org/article/the-eviction-ban-worked-but-its-almost-over-some-landlords-are-getting-ready (Aug 2020); lookup app: https://projects.propublica.org/covid-evictions/; how-to: https://www.propublica.org/article/can-you-be-evicted-during-coronavirus-heres-how-to-find-out
- **Partner/awards**: ProPublica original (data team incl. Haru Coryne, Ellis Simani; Princeton Eviction Lab data partnership).
- **What they found**:
  - Pre-pandemic, federally backed properties accounted for over a third of eviction filings in Atlanta and Houston (~7,700 households/month combined); during the CARES moratorium (May–June 2020) their filings fell to under 200/month (~5% of filings) — the ban measurably worked where it applied.
  - Bulk filings (5+ evictions per property) collapsed from 1,000+ buildings (~12,000 households) to ~100 properties (<1,200 households).
  - Coverage was geographically unequal: the federal ban reached 40%+ of pre-pandemic evictions in metro Atlanta but only ~20% in St. Louis and ~10% in Milwaukee; ProPublica separately reported landlords in at least four states (GA, OK, TX, FL) moving to evict 100+ households in violation of the ban.
  - The lookup app let any tenant check whether their building was federally backed — operationalizing the legal test courts weren't applying.
- **Finding type(s)**: *moratorium-defiance* (filings inside a prohibited window at covered properties); *protection-coverage inequality* (a legal shield whose reach varies by geography/financing structure, measured); *rights-operationalization tool* (turning an obscure eligibility join into a public lookup).
- **Evidence & sources**:
  - Local eviction dockets — collected from court websites in Georgia, Tennessee, Florida, Texas and via Princeton's Eviction Lab across ~10 cities.
  - Federally backed property records — Fannie/Freddie loan lookups, HUD/FHA-subsidized property lists (the CARES-covered universe).
  - Digital parcel/map files — geocoding backbone for the join.
  - Random-sample manual validation of matches; acknowledgment that federally backed single-family homes lack public data [stated limitation].
- **Detection signature**: *geocode JOIN coverage-list inside a prohibition window* — stated methodology (paraphrased from the article): eviction cases were geocoded and matched to parcels via digital map files; properties known to be covered by the CARES moratorium were matched to the same parcel base; the two were cross-referenced, then compared across pre-pandemic vs moratorium months and by landlord size. Filings at covered properties during the ban = violations; the pre/post differential at covered vs uncovered properties = causal-style evidence the ban worked.
- **Corroboration structure**: docket data -> parcel match -> federal coverage lists -> random-inspection validation -> named-landlord confirmation in follow-ups.
- **Methodology notes**: matching method stated inline in the article [stated]; app methodology on the project page.
- **Official impact**: fed the national 2020–21 eviction-policy debate (the CDC ban followed weeks later); congressional oversight of pandemic corporate-landlord evictions proceeded on the same docket-join logic [contextual; direct causation not claimed].
- **Generalization**: the template for policing any conduct pause — sanctions windows, hiring freezes, trading blackouts, stays of collection, disaster anti-price-gouging periods. Generic detector: (1) define the covered-entity list; (2) collect the activity stream (filings, trades, listings); (3) join on a stable key (parcel, CUSIP, license number); (4) filter to the prohibited window; every match is a violation candidate, and the covered-vs-uncovered differential measures whether the rule bites.

### 3. Transferable pattern candidates

**Pattern: Conditioned-Benefit Reconciliation.** Whenever an actor receives a public benefit conditioned on an obligation, two official tables must exist: the recipient roll and the compliance register. The scheme is the anti-join — entities present in the first, absent (or false) in the second — and its price is the benefit amount flowing to the non-compliant set. ProPublica ran this on NYC tax breaks vs rent registrations (50,000 units), LIHTC credits vs affordability terms, and CDBG grants vs fair-housing certifications; enforcement agencies almost never run it themselves, which is why the gap persists for decades. Minimum data: recipient list with IDs/amounts; the obligation's register or filing repository; a joinable key (parcel, EIN, grantee ID). Recognition cue in any domain: a subsidy, license, exemption, or accreditation whose conditions are self-certified and whose regulator cannot produce an enforcement ledger — ask who has ever lost the benefit; if the answer is roughly nobody, run the join.

**Pattern: Certification Theater Audit.** Official scoring/inspection regimes drift into theater: scores stay high while the measured reality decays, because the score is gameable, the inspectorate is under-resourced, or failure is politically costly. Detect by joining certification outcomes to any independent contemporaneous measurement of the same subjects — municipal citations, complaints, litigation, sensor/ground data, insider accounts — and by watching for failure-rate step-changes when scoring rules tighten (retroactive proof of prior inflation). ProPublica's REAC work is the archetype; the same audit fits nursing-home stars, financial audits, safety certifications, sanctions-compliance attestations. Minimum data: the certification file with subject IDs, dates, scores; one independent condition stream. Recognition cue: a regulator that both funds/depends on and grades the same entities, plus anecdotes of subjects preparing for inspections rather than for safety.

**Pattern: Protected-Inventory Leak Detection.** A legally protected stock (affordable units, controlled goods, preserved assets) leaks into the open market, where sellers must advertise. The protected roster is a watchlist; marketplaces are the sensor. Continuously match roster entries against commercial listing surfaces (fuzzy matching over names, addresses, imagery when platforms mask locations), confirm candidates in official records, and — the strongest variant — look for a second government registry (tax, licensing) that officially records the prohibited use. Minimum data: the protected-asset register; scraped/collected listing data; ideally the tax/licensing registry of the prohibited activity. Recognition cue: any protection regime with no assigned enforcement staff and an active resale/rental market for the protected asset class.

**Pattern: Prohibition-Window Activity Scan.** When law pauses an activity (moratorium, stay, blackout, sanction), the violation set is computable: collect the activity stream (court filings, transactions, listings), join to the covered-entity list on a stable key, and filter to the window. Two ProPublica variants: direct violations (evictions filed at CARES-covered parcels; 100+ households in four states) and carve-out exploitation (HOAs foreclosing through the mortgage-moratorium gap — 730+ cases). The covered-vs-uncovered differential doubles as a policy-effectiveness measurement. Minimum data: dated activity records with actor/asset IDs; the coverage definition/list; the window dates. Recognition cue: any emergency pause — immediately ask which actor classes are outside it and who is filing anyway.

**Pattern: Process-Profiteer Accounting.** Some enforcement/collection processes exist economically for their intermediaries: fees dwarf the underlying debt, and the intermediary is paid regardless of outcome. Build the case population (docket harvest by party-name patterns), rank the specialized firms by volume, and compute fee-to-principal ratios and outcome chains (how many small debts became forfeitures). ProPublica's Colorado HOA work ($1,515 debt -> $25,774 to keep the house; seven firms with 100+ filings each) mirrors tax-lien mills, guardianship billing, debt-collection suits, and civil forfeiture. Minimum data: bulk docket with parties and dates; a sample of case files exposing fee schedules; outcome records (sales, judgments). Recognition cue: a repeat-player bar on one side of thousands of near-identical small-dollar cases against unrepresented parties — then check what the industry lobbies to protect (fee caps were the provision stripped from Colorado's reform), because that reveals where the money is.
