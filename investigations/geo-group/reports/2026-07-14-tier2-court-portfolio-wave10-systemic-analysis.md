# GEO CourtListener/RECAP portfolio: systemic legal and economic mechanisms

**Date:** July 14, 2026  
**Profile / thread:** `geo-group` / 113  
**Analysis runs:** systemic-analysis #116; generate-hunches #119  
**Scope:** bounded Tier-2 analysis of completed, authenticated case reports and verified findings; no new merits search

## Result

The recurring mechanism supported by the audited portfolio is **issue-specific allocation of federal and contractor control**, not blanket sovereign direction and not coordinated GEO–DHS conduct.

Courts repeatedly asked what ICE actually directed, what GEO retained discretion to do, and which physical areas or operational functions each actor controlled. The answers differed by issue:

- In *Menocal*, the district court found that ICE neither directed the challenged sanitation labor nor imposed a $1-per-day pay ceiling, defeating GEO's Yearsley and government-contractor defenses (#12557). The Supreme Court later held only that Yearsley is a merits defense rather than immediately appealable sovereign immunity (#12551).
- In *Novoa*, the district court found the contract did not impose a $1 ceiling and GEO controlled detainee wages (#12780). The court entered nonfinal California wage and UCL liability, dismissed derivative-immunity and preemption defenses, and later stayed the action while acknowledging that the federal TVPA claims did not depend on those defenses (#12779–#12781).
- In the NWIPC inspection litigation, the Ninth Circuit held that Washington regulated GEO rather than the federal government and rejected field and obstacle preemption (#12741–#12744). On remand, the district court divided access by area: GEO had to admit inspectors to detainee-facing portions while ICE-controlled administrative and medical areas remained carved out (#12737).
- At Delaney Hall, GEO invoked federal-officer removal, Yearsley, intergovernmental immunity, and preemption as party positions (#12761). The United States separately said ICE expected compliance with applicable building codes and was prepared to schedule inspection (#12762). Access occurred, the emergency motion was withdrawn, and no merits judgment followed (#12763).

This cross-case pattern is meaningful but largely already registered. Earlier case forensics identified contractor discretion as outcome-determinative, and completed runs #94/#95 tested the adjacent litigation-to-contract-continuity theory against the existing #335/#336 competition. One newly authenticated economic record does warrant a bounded synthesis finding: ICE denied in full GEO's three June 2018 defense-cost requests across Aurora, Northwest, and Adelanto, totaling $2,662,654.03 (#13004–#13010, #13014, synthesized in #13016). The current pass creates that one finding, but no new hypothesis, lead, tag, entity, or connection.

## Corpus and evidence discipline

That universe is a discovery denominator, not an adverse-outcome rate.

Only reports whose SHA-256 values matched the coverage audit's source manifest were used for case propositions. The analyzed clusters were:

## 1. Contractor control and sovereign direction

### Detainee labor

The labor cases reject a categorical inference that an ICE contract converts every GEO practice into federal direction.

*Nwauzor/Washington* produced an affirmed lower-court judgment applying Washington minimum-wage law to work essential to facility operations (#12402), with $17.287 million in back pay, $5.950 million in unjust-enrichment relief, and an injunction (#12553). The same opinion reported GEO's estimate that ending the program would require about 85 outside full-time employees (#12550) and a ten-year contract minimum of $700 million with substantial annual facility gross profit (#12554). Those economic facts explain the program's operational importance; they do not create federal authorization for the wage practice.

*Menocal* is the clearest direction test. ICE required a voluntary work program and reimbursed at a nominal level, but the district court found that the agency did not direct the challenged labor and did not prohibit higher pay. It also held GEO's standardized agreement unenforceable (#12559). These are nonfinal district merits rulings within an active case; they are not a final TVPA verdict or damages award.

*Novoa* supplies an independent California contract interpretation. The court found that GEO controlled wages and the contract contained no upper limit (#12780). It entered nonfinal California minimum-wage and UCL liability (#12779), while leaving forced-labor claims for trial and later staying the case (#12781). The stay links the federal defenses procedurally to *Nwauzor* but does not transform the disputes into independent corroboration of agency coordination.

### Inspection and access

The inspection cases reveal the same granular approach in physical space.

The Ninth Circuit's *Inslee* decision rejected GEO's claim that the Washington scheme directly regulated the federal government and rejected field and obstacle preemption (#12741, #12743, #12744). Its comparator holding treated other private civil-detention settings—not prisons and jails—as the relevant baseline (#12742). The 2026 preliminary injunction then separated GEO-controlled detainee-facing areas from ICE-controlled administrative and medical areas (#12737).

Delaney Hall is a useful negative control. GEO pleaded the same suite of contractor defenses, but the public root docket contains no merits ruling (#12761, #12765). In the related City case, DOJ's Statement of Interest expected code compliance and offered ICE-facilitated inspection (#12762). Inspection occurred; Newark withdrew emergency relief and the case moved into mediation without a judgment (#12763). Repeated GEO advocacy therefore cannot be counted as a repeated holding or a unified government position.

The Third Circuit contract-ban comparator further limits generalization. *CoreCivic v. Murphy* invalidated New Jersey's ban in a federal-only contracting market but expressly left neutral conditions on contractors open (#12764). Protection of the federal government's ability to contract is analytically distinct from immunity from ordinary health, safety, construction, or inspection rules.

## 2. Agency participation: selective alignment, not a single portfolio position

The authenticated reports show federal participation that changes with the issue:

- the United States supported GEO on a comparator issue in *Inslee* but did not adopt all of GEO's theories;
- a United States amicus filing supported further review in the *Nwauzor* appellate line;
- DOJ at Delaney Hall expected local-code compliance and proposed scheduled inspection; and
- ICE and OPLA personnel were aware of GEO's 2018 defense-cost request and planned engagement, but ICE contracting officers still denied all three requests; and
- no authenticated agency filing establishes that DHS directed a common litigation position across the portfolio.

This variation matters. It is consistent with ordinary agency litigation judgments and issue-specific federal interests. It is inconsistent with describing GEO's recurring pleadings as proof that DHS coordinated the full defense strategy. Common legal labels—Yearsley, federal-officer removal, preemption, and intergovernmental immunity—are expected when the same contractor litigates state regulation around federal facilities.

## 3. Indemnity, reimbursement, and defense-cost allocation

The completed Hartel retrieval answers the bounded 2018 economic question: GEO asked to shift voluntary-work-program litigation-defense costs to the government, and ICE denied the three requests in full.

At the pleading stage, the court held plaintiffs adequately alleged scienter for GEO's statements about pending-lawsuit costs (#12562). DE 63 later limited the claim to pre–July 17, 2019 publication events (#12815–#12816). The case ended in a $3 million settlement, express denial of wrongdoing, settlement-only class certification, and dismissal with prejudice (#12577, #12593, #12594, #12817). Those securities rulings do not adjudicate contract entitlement, but the underlying correspondence and contracting records are now independently authenticated.

GEO's May 30, 2018 request sought DOJ defense and reimbursement of legal costs. By June 21, ICE had denied in full three requests for equitable adjustment: $1,928,433.38 for *Menocal* at Aurora, $595,160.69 for *Washington/Chen* at Northwest, and $139,059.96 for *Novoa* at Adelanto (#13004–#13007). ICE characterized the suits as challenges to GEO's performance, found no constructive change, treated legal fees as non-cognizable under the requested theory, and also found the quantum submissions inadequate. The ICE response routed GEO to DOJ contacts but did not promise representation or payment (#13008).

The contract record is consistent with contractor-borne litigation risk in the sampled vehicles. The Northwest contract expressly made GEO responsible for litigation and its cost (#13009). The Aurora and Adelanto instruments routed changes and disputes through contracting-officer processes rather than establishing automatic reimbursement (#13010, #13014). Internal correspondence separately shows leadership awareness, planned engagement with OPLA, and a tracked Homan response (#13015); it does not establish a favorable agency decision or coordinated legal representation.

Finding #13016 therefore records a narrow systemic result: one coordinated three-vehicle request met a common batch denial and an express contractor-cost clause at Northwest. It does **not** prove a permanent bar, a portfolio-wide policy, or the absence of later nonpublic reconsideration, payment, a compensating modification, a Contract Disputes Act appeal, or DOJ representation. Lead #63588 is completed. Human actions #47 and #50 still seek current NWIPC and Delaney Hall clauses; creating another hypothesis or acquisition lead would duplicate or outrun the evidence.

## 4. Inspection resistance and remediation

The portfolio does not support one uniform obstruction mechanism.

- NWIPC produced adjudicated access orders and a facility-area carveout.
- A separate Washington L&I access matter ended in a stipulated permanent-access arrangement, according to the authenticated *Inslee* report.
- Delaney Hall inspection ultimately occurred, the emergency motion was withdrawn, and competing reports contained both cited maintenance/permit issues and favorable fire/elevator observations (#12763).

These contexts show contested access boundaries. They do not establish a common corporate directive to obstruct inspection, and they do not establish that all disputes were resolved through ordinary compliance. The missing contracts and current appellate records remain necessary to decide whether the same access clause or internal policy recurs.

## 5. FCA and organizational attribution controls

The FCA cases warn against treating a common caption as a common GEO–DHS mechanism.

*Roycroft* concerned Ohio Medicaid provider claims, not an ICE/DHS procurement. The Sixth Circuit held that six of seven claims were presented but the relator failed to plead a specific falsity with Rule 9(b) particularity (#12768, #12770). The alleged conduct and Roycroft's employment predated GEO's acquisition of Cornell, so the appellate caption does not prove GEO parent-level operational responsibility during 2008–2009 (#12773).

*Burciaga* is a materially incomplete FCA record. Later federal sources preserve summary-judgment-stage scienter and concealment/materiality quotations (#12784–#12785), and statistical records show a later voluntary dismissal (#12787). The complaint, payer, contract, intervention posture, terminal terms, and payment remain unavailable (#12788). It cannot be assigned to ICE/DHS or counted as a procurement-fraud outcome.

These are attribution hazards—not evidence that GEO deliberately compartmentalized liability through subsidiaries. A coordination or intent claim would require transaction-specific corporate records, operating responsibility, and common internal routing evidence.

## 6. Procurement litigation controls

GEO's 2011 Brooklyn reentry-center protest concerned BOP, not ICE. The Court of Federal Claims denied the TRO after finding no demonstrated likelihood of success, reported no indication that a former GEO employee violated the Procurement Integrity Act, and later dismissed the action with prejudice following GEO's voluntary dismissal (#12560, #12574, #12592, #12614). This is ordinary protest activity and a preliminary-stage loss, not a DHS coordination event.

The Salus/CSI case is an independent DHS comparator. It contains explicit public documentation of a potential organizational-conflict waiver and urgency rationale (#12970), while the court dismissed CSI on standing and alternative interested-party/prejudice grounds (#12971). The reviewed record established no Salus–GEO relationship or pre-award political-favor bridge (#12975). The comparator demonstrates what an agency procurement judgment can look like when it is documented; it does not supply a GEO mechanism.

## 7. Contract continuity and consequence overlap

The existing #335/#336 competition already tests whether ICE contract design and monitoring insulate GEO from proportional financial consequences or whether the episodes reflect ordinary remedies and readiness purchasing.

The current litigation portfolio adds one historical allocation result but no evidence that distinguishes the broader #335/#336 competition. ICE's 2018 denial kept the sampled defense costs with GEO at that decision point (#13016). Folkston documents attempted but unenforced staffing penalties (#12425), Northwest retains an open minimum recommendation and repeat deficiencies (#12819, #12822), South Texas records an ordinary readiness response (#12818), and Adelanto supplies a later no-findings control (#12801). Separately, the controlled event study found no litigation milestone with a statistically discriminatory 90-day award-action pattern after correction (#12619).

Award-action continuity is therefore descriptive. It cannot reveal CPARS, responsibility decisions, deductions, cure notices, option rationales, occupancy need, invoice treatment, or nonpublic mitigation.

## ACH assessment

The [ACH artifact](./2026-07-14-tier2-court-portfolio-wave10-ach.json) compares three explanations:

1. issue-specific allocation of federal and contractor control;
2. ordinary fact-specific federal-contractor advocacy; and
3. a coordinated GEO–DHS insulation strategy.

The first two tie for **least evidence against** and are compatible with one another. The coordination alternative is less consistent with the record because multiple courts found GEO retained the relevant discretion, the United States did not adopt a uniform position, and the authenticated 2018 cost record shows denial rather than insulation. Agency access and engagement followed by adverse contracting decisions are more consistent with issue-specific administration than with a unified protective strategy. None of the alternatives is confirmed.

## Novelty and database decision

Seven candidate patterns were tested:

1. granular control allocation across labor and inspection;
2. repeated sovereign-direction and preemption advocacy;
3. agency-position compartmentalization;
4. defense-cost shifting or reimbursement;
5. inspection/access resistance;
6. organizational compartmentalization; and
7. litigation-to-contract consequence insulation.

The first two are already explicit in the case findings and prior synthesis. Agency-position variation lacks evidence of deliberate compartmentalization. The defense-cost candidate now passes the novelty gate only as bounded 2018 synthesis finding #13016; it does not pass the hunch gate because the record shows requests and denials, not a recurring successful cost-shift mechanism. Inspection lacks a uniform outcome. Organizational compartmentalization fails the intent and attribution threshold. The contract-consequence theory duplicates #335/#336 and completed runs #94/#95.

Systemic-analysis run #116 therefore created **one** synthesis finding (#13016). Generate-hunches run #119 created **zero** new hypotheses or leads; neither run created tags, entities, or connections. The [novelty report](./2026-07-14-tier2-court-portfolio-wave10-novelty-report.json) records all seven candidates and their reconsideration triggers.

## Premortem and reconsideration trigger

Assume this conclusion is wrong. The most likely failure is that later agency or payment records reverse the authenticated June 2018 denials. A second failure is that apparently divergent DOJ positions were tactically coordinated at a higher level not reflected in the filed briefs. A third is treating issue-specific contract clauses as independent when they derive from one standardized ICE template.

The fastest residual check is for a later reconsideration, compensating modification, voucher, Contract Disputes Act appeal, or DOJ representation after the denials, plus the current NWIPC and Delaney contracts. Reconsider a new coordination hypothesis only if primary records show repeated common direction or favorable economic treatment across at least three independent facility contexts after controlling for legal posture and standard contract language.
