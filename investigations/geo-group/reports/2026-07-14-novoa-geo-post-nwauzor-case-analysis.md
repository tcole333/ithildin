## Bottom line

*Novoa* is **not allegation-only**. On January 25, 2022, the district court entered a developed but nonfinal partial summary-judgment order holding GEO liable under California's minimum-wage and Unfair Competition laws. It also dismissed GEO's derivative-sovereign-immunity and preemption defenses and its declaratory counterclaim. The court denied GEO summary judgment on the federal and California forced-labor claims because material jury questions remained.

The case nevertheless has no final judgment. On March 31, 2022, the court stayed the entire action pending the consolidated *Nwauzor/Washington* appeals. The CourtListener docket was statistically marked terminated under JS-6, but the court retained the case, required reports every 90 days, and later clarified that the stay displaced the scheduled trial. Through ECF 610 on June 15, 2026, the fully paginated docket shows no order lifting the stay, trial, verdict, settlement, dismissal, damages or restitution order, injunction, or final judgment.

The current appellate backdrop is clear from primary sources. The Ninth Circuit affirmed against GEO on January 16, 2025 and denied panel and en banc rehearing on August 13, 2025. GEO filed certiorari petition No. 25-828 on January 9, 2026. On May 18, the Supreme Court invited the Solicitor General to express the views of the United States; as of July 14, no SG brief, grant, or denial appeared on the official docket.

What is **not** established is the parties' express post-rehearing explanation for leaving *Novoa* stayed. CourtListener indexes ECF 607-610 but does not provide their PDFs or text. The certiorari petition and CVSG are the obvious procedural explanation for continued quarterly reporting, but until those filings are retrieved, that remains an inference rather than a quoted *Novoa* party position.

For cross-case analysis, *Novoa* should be classified as a **distinct, nonfinal adjudicated California state-regulatory context**. It supplies an actual district-court liability ruling and ICE-contract interpretation, but it must not be described as a final adverse judgment, forced-labor liability determination, damages award, or completed case.

## 1. Claims, classes, and allegation boundaries

The September 16, 2019 third amended complaint asserted seven causes of action arising from detention at the Adelanto ICE Processing Center: California minimum wage, unjust enrichment, California UCL, California forced-labor law, federal forced labor, attempted forced labor, and retaliation. It challenged the paid Voluntary Work Program and allegedly uncompensated housing-unit sanitation work. Threats, coercion, and resulting harms in the complaint remain allegations except where the January 2022 order expressly adjudicated an issue.

The court certified an Adelanto Wage Class, Adelanto Forced Labor Class, and Nationwide Housing Unit Sanitation Policy Class on November 26, 2019. On September 30, 2021, it kept the Adelanto Forced Labor Class but decertified the Nationwide HUSP Class. The partial-summary-judgment order therefore addressed a materially developed class record, not just pleading sufficiency.

## 2. January 25, 2022 partial merits ruling

ECF 542 supplies the central correction to the earlier litigation summary.

### California wage and UCL liability

The court found no genuine dispute that GEO controlled detainee wages, hours, or working conditions under the California test. It granted plaintiffs summary judgment and denied GEO summary judgment on Count I, the California minimum-wage claim. Because GEO's payment of $1 per day violated the California minimum-wage requirement, the court also granted plaintiffs summary judgment on Count III, the derivative UCL claim.

These are liability holdings, not a final damages or restitution award. The order left limitations, prospective-relief, third-party-liability, and other issues unresolved.

### Unjust enrichment and forced labor

The court denied both sides summary judgment on unjust enrichment. It reasoned that a jury could find GEO gained profits or competitive advantage by relying on detainee labor, but the competing evidence could also support GEO's theory that hiring additional outside labor would increase reimbursable costs and nominal profit dollars.

The court denied GEO summary judgment on the California and federal trafficking claims. It held that a reasonable jury could find that threats of segregation or other sanctions compelled uncompensated work beyond personal housekeeping, but it did not decide that GEO used forced labor. The forced-labor merits, scienter, and damages remained for trial.

### Federal defenses and counterclaim

The order dismissed GEO's derivative-sovereign-immunity and preemption defenses and denied GEO summary judgment on intergovernmental immunity. It also dismissed GEO's declaratory counterclaim. Other defenses, including statutes of limitation, third-party liability involving ICE and Adelanto, and unauthorized-work arguments, were left unresolved.

## 3. ICE contract and detainee-work findings

The order recorded the following contract history:

- In May 2011, ICE used an intergovernmental service agreement with the City of Adelanto, which contracted with GEO.
- After the City terminated that arrangement, ICE entered a temporary direct bridge contract with GEO on June 25, 2019.
- ICE entered a new direct contract on December 19, 2019, with a stated performance period from December 20, 2019 through December 19, 2034.

That 2034 end date is a fact reported in the 2022 judicial order; it is not proof that every option, modification, scope term, or current operational arrangement remains unchanged in 2026.

The wage-ceiling analysis is more probative. The direct contract said detainee labor “will shall [sic] be paid $1 day,” but the court found that wording ambiguous. ICE's Rule 30(b)(6) witness testified that ICE did not prohibit paying more and that the contract contained no upper limit. GEO also paid more than $1 per day at other facilities. The court therefore rejected GEO's interpretation and found GEO controlled Adelanto detainee wages.

This holding reinforces the cross-case distinction between an ICE reimbursement line and a contractual cap on contractor compensation. The contract relationship did not establish that ICE directed every GEO wage or labor practice.

## 4. Why the court stayed the case

ECF 561 expressly identifies the consolidated *Nwauzor* and *Washington* appeals as the reason for the stay. The district court reasoned that a Ninth Circuit ruling for GEO on intergovernmental immunity, derivative sovereign immunity, or preemption could moot a jury verdict or portions of ECF 542 and force the court and parties to litigate the same issues twice.

The court also acknowledged limits to that overlap:

- Washington and California wage laws differ.
- The Ninth Circuit could decide the appeal narrowly under Washington law.
- GEO's affirmative defenses did not affect the federal TVPA claims.

Despite those differences, the court concluded that precedential appellate rulings and litigation efficiency justified staying the entire case.

The same day, a separate order continued trial to October 4, 2022, creating an apparent conflict. GEO sought clarification in ECF 572. On August 31, 2022, ECF 584 granted clarification and denied pending trial-related motions as moot. The docket thereafter lists no trial date. Statistical “termination” on March 31, 2022 therefore means administrative closure, not final merits disposition.

## 5. Post-stay timeline and current posture

Available reports trace the related appeals:

- October 6, 2022: Ninth Circuit oral argument.
- March 7, 2023: Ninth Circuit certified Washington-law questions to the Washington Supreme Court.
- March 17, 2023: plaintiffs filed a report urging the district court to vacate the stay and set trial; the docket shows no resulting stay-lifting order.
- December 21, 2023: Washington Supreme Court held that detainees working at the private facility were employees under the Washington Minimum Wage Act and that the government-institutions exemption did not apply.
- January 16, 2025: a divided Ninth Circuit affirmed the judgments and rejected GEO's intergovernmental-immunity, preemption, and derivative-sovereign-immunity defenses.
- June 16, 2025: the last downloadable *Novoa* report said mandate had not issued because GEO's rehearing petition remained pending; the United States had filed an amicus brief supporting rehearing.
- August 13, 2025: the Ninth Circuit denied panel and en banc rehearing.
- September 15 and December 15, 2025, and March 16 and June 15, 2026: *Novoa* joint status reports were docketed as ECF 607-610, but CourtListener lacks their documents.
- January 9, 2026: GEO filed certiorari petition No. 25-828.
- May 18, 2026: Supreme Court issued a CVSG.
- July 14, 2026: the official Supreme Court docket remained pending, and the *Novoa* docket still contained no stay-lifting or final merits order.

The post-August 2025 causal conclusion must be bounded. The missing reports likely discuss certiorari and the CVSG, but the available *Novoa* text does not prove that. Human action #57 requests the exact filings.

## Learnings

- [Methodology] A statistically terminated docket can contain a live stayed action with years of status reports; JS-6 is not a final-judgment marker.
- [Methodology] “No final liability judgment” must not be collapsed into “no liability ruling.” Partial summary judgment can establish liability while remedies and other claims remain unresolved.
- [Methodology] Denial of a defendant's forced-labor summary-judgment motion establishes trial sufficiency, not plaintiff-side liability.
- [Methodology] An ICE reimbursement amount is not necessarily a contractual wage ceiling; party intent and agency testimony can establish contractor discretion.
- [Source quality] CourtListener supplied unusually complete docket structure and decisive PDFs, but missing ECF 607-610 prevents direct attribution of the post-rehearing stay rationale.
