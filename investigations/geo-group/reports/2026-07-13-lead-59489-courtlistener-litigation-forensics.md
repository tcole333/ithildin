# GEO Group litigation forensics: detainee labor, medical care, conditions, and civil rights

**Lead:** 59489  
**Profile / thread:** `geo-group` / 113  
**Snapshot date:** 2026-07-13  
**Primary research system:** CourtListener opinions, RECAP dockets and filings, and FJC metadata; the current Supreme Court posture of *GEO Group v. Nwauzor*, No. 25-828, was checked on the official Supreme Court docket.

## Bottom line

The most consequential GEO litigation in this record is concentrated in two detainee-labor lines. *Nwauzor/Washington* produced an affirmed Washington minimum-wage judgment of $17,287,063.05 for the detainee class, $5,950,340 in unjust-enrichment relief for Washington, and an injunction against operating the Tacoma Voluntary Work Program below Washington minimum wage. *Menocal* has not yet produced a final merits judgment, but the district court rejected GEO's government-contractor defenses, held that the challenged $1-per-day agreement was unenforceable, and the Supreme Court later held that Yearsley is a merits defense rather than immediately appealable immunity.

The government-contract record matters because the courts repeatedly separated what ICE actually required from choices GEO made while performing the contracts. In *Menocal*, the district court found that ICE neither directed nor required the challenged labor or a $1-per-day ceiling. In *Nwauzor*, the Ninth Circuit rejected GEO's federal-immunity and preemption defenses while affirming application of Washington wage law to work supporting the private facility's operation. The existence of an ICE contract therefore did not itself immunize every contractor-created labor practice.

Medical-care litigation shows a different remedial structure. *Minneci v. Pollard* bars an implied Bivens damages remedy for improper medical care by private federal-prison employees when state tort law offers an alternative. The currently active *Reid* death case proceeds principally through Virginia negligence theories after the federal Count Seven claims were dismissed. That split is important: contractor status can defeat a federal implied cause of action without eliminating state-law exposure.

## 1. Tacoma detainee labor: Nwauzor and Washington

### Contract economics and labor substitution

The Ninth Circuit's January 16, 2025 opinion describes a ten-year ICE contract beginning in 2015 with a minimum value of $700 million. It reports annual facility gross profit between $18.6 million and $23.5 million from 2010 through 2018 and general net margins of 16% to 19%. Those figures are court-reported evidence, not an independent accounting reconstruction. See finding 12554 and [CourtListener cluster 10315081](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10315081/ugochukwu-nwauzor-v-the-geo-group-inc/).

The same opinion records GEO's estimate that ending the Tacoma Voluntary Work Program would require approximately 85 additional full-time outside employees. Finding 12550 was narrowed during audit to that exact supported proposition. The opinion elsewhere describes work in meal preparation, kitchen sanitation, janitorial services, repairs, waste handling, and laundry, but those service categories are reported here as opinion background rather than folded into the finding's quoted claim.

### Holdings and remedies

The Washington Supreme Court answered the certified state-law question by holding that detained workers at this privately owned and operated facility could be employees under Washington's Minimum Wage Act and that the government-institutions exemption did not cover the private facility. The Ninth Circuit then affirmed against GEO's intergovernmental-immunity, preemption, and derivative-sovereign-immunity arguments.

The resulting remedies were substantial:

- $17,287,063.05 in back pay to the detainee class.
- $5,950,340 in unjust-enrichment relief to Washington.
- An injunction against operating the program without paying Washington minimum wage.

These figures and the injunction are preserved in finding 12553. The principal wage holding is also anchored by pre-existing finding 12402, which quotes the appellate description of work involving tasks essential to facility operations.

### Current Supreme Court posture

GEO filed a petition for certiorari on January 9, 2026, docketed as *The GEO Group, Inc. v. Nwauzor*, No. 25-828. The petition was distributed for the May 14 conference, and on May 18 the Court invited the Solicitor General to express the views of the United States. The official docket had no grant or denial entry when checked July 13, 2026. Finding 12591 records this posture from the [Supreme Court docket](https://www.supremecourt.gov/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-828.html). The pending petition does not itself replace the lower-court judgment.

## 2. Aurora detainee labor: Menocal

### Certified classes, not final liability

The Tenth Circuit's 2018 decision affirmed certification of two classes: a federal TVPA class concerning allegedly compelled common-area cleaning and a Colorado unjust-enrichment class concerning GEO's $1-per-day Voluntary Work Program. That ruling did not decide whether GEO ultimately violated either body of law. Finding 12556 preserves both the policy description and the class-certification posture.

### Contractor discretion versus ICE direction

In the October 18, 2022 summary-judgment order, the district court found that ICE neither directed nor required GEO to compel the challenged labor or limit VWP compensation to $1 per day. It therefore rejected GEO's Yearsley and government-contractor defenses. Finding 12557 was audited and narrowed to those exact quoted holdings.

The order also held the standardized $1-per-day agreement procedurally and substantively unconscionable and unenforceable. It cited detainees' unequal bargaining position, detention status, lack of a meaningful alternative, and a flat daily rate that did not vary with the work. That contract ruling is finding 12559. It permitted the unjust-enrichment theory to proceed; it was not a final damages judgment.

The record also indicates that ICE reimbursed the facility at the $1-per-day level while another GEO facility paid as much as $4 per day, a distinction supporting the court's analysis that reimbursement was not a contractual wage ceiling. This is background from document 380 and should not be treated as a separate adjudication of damages.

### Supreme Court and current district-court posture

On February 25, 2026, the Supreme Court held that Yearsley supplies a defense to liability, not immunity from suit, and that denial of the defense therefore was not immediately appealable. The Court did not decide the TVPA or unjust-enrichment merits. Finding 12551 captures that holding.

The underlying District of Colorado case, 1:14-cv-02887, remains active. GEO filed a new partial-summary-judgment/judgment-on-the-pleadings motion on April 6, 2026, with briefing continuing in May. Lead 59578 should monitor the post-*Menocal* merits result, class scope, trial posture, and any settlement or damages order.

## 3. Adelanto and the nationwide policy theory: Novoa

The operative complaint in *Novoa v. GEO Group*, C.D. Cal. 5:17-cv-02514, alleges California minimum-wage and unfair-competition violations, unjust enrichment, and federal and California forced-labor violations. It challenges paid work at $1 per day and uncompensated housing-unit sanitation work at Adelanto, and it pleads a nationwide Housing Unit Sanitation Policy class. The complaint further alleges threats of segregation, referral to ICE, criminal prosecution, or other harm. These are allegations, not findings of fact.

The case was stayed on March 31, 2022 pending the related *Nwauzor/Washington* appeals. The docket was statistically terminated but the court required periodic status reports. The June 2025 report said the parties were still awaiting action in the Ninth Circuit proceedings, and RECAP records another joint status report on June 15, 2026. Finding 12582 records that live procedural trail; no final *Novoa* liability ruling was located.

The pending Supreme Court petition in *Nwauzor* supplies the likely current reason for continued status reporting, but the unavailable June 2026 filing prevents treating that inference as a quoted party statement. Lead 59678 should obtain the latest report and the eventual order lifting or continuing the stay.

## 4. Medical care and death

### Reid v. GEO Group: active state-law exposure

*Reid*, E.D. Va. 3:24-cv-00309, arises from allegations that Lawrenceville personnel failed to provide adequate emergency care to Damian Hernandez before his death on November 6, 2020. The May 15, 2025 motion-to-dismiss opinion accepted the complaint's facts only for pleading purposes. It dismissed the direct Monell/failure-to-train theory against GEO but allowed negligence and gross-negligence respondeat-superior theories under Counts One through Six to continue.

The procedural posture changed again in 2026:

- March 23: Count Seven was dismissed against all remaining defendants.
- May 15: the court resolved Daubert motions, lifted the stay, opened dispositive briefing, and referred the matter for settlement.
- June 2-5: defendants, including GEO, filed summary-judgment motions.
- July 13: the court had scheduled a settlement conference for 2:00 p.m.; this report does not assume an outcome.

Finding 12578 records the exact posture. Lead 59676 should collect the settlement result and summary-judgment rulings. The court has not yet entered a final finding that the alleged medical conduct caused Hernandez's death.

### Minneci v. Pollard: the private-contractor remedy rule

*Minneci* arose from medical-care allegations against Wackenhut employees at a privately operated federal prison. The Supreme Court did not decide whether the allegations were true. It held that when alleged improper medical care falls within traditional state tort law and state remedies are available, a federal prisoner cannot obtain an implied Bivens remedy against the private employees. Finding 12579 preserves the controlling quotation.

The practical significance is remedial fragmentation: a private contractor's personnel may face state negligence or malpractice liability while avoiding the federal implied damages action that would apply in some suits against federal employees.

### Newsome false-positive screen

*Newsome v. GEO Group*, D.N.M. 2:12-cv-00733, appeared in the broad medical/death result set but is not a medical-care or custodial-death case. It involved a prisoner's First Amendment retaliation, grievance, public-records, discipline, and transfer allegations. The September 29, 2015 final opinion dismissed most claims for failure to exhaust and granted defendants summary judgment on the remaining claims, dismissing them with prejudice. It belongs in the civil-rights screening log, not the death cluster.

## 5. Civil commitment, sanitation, housing, and speech

### Bilal v. GEO Care

In the revised 2020 Eleventh Circuit opinion, the court held that Jamaal Bilal sufficiently alleged Fourteenth Amendment claims by asserting that transport officers denied any bathroom stop during a 600-mile trip, causing him to sit in his feces for roughly 300 miles, and that he was housed for a month in a county jail under punitive conditions while civilly committed. The ruling was at the pleading stage and did not establish liability. The parties filed a joint stipulation of dismissal with prejudice on August 11, 2021; the accessible docket does not disclose settlement terms. See finding 12580.

### Pesci v. Budz

The Eleventh Circuit's 2019 *Pesci* opinion upheld the Florida Civil Commitment Center's 2010 ban on the *Duck Soup* newsletter and 2006 printing-page limit under the modified Turner standard. It treated civil commitment as rehabilitative rather than punitive while deferring to documented security and resource-conservation rationales. The holding did not validate the truth of accusations in the newsletters. See finding 12581.

## Cross-case analysis

### 1. Contract language and contractor discretion are outcome-determinative

The labor cases turn less on the abstract existence of an ICE contract than on whether the challenged practice was actually directed by the government. *Menocal* is clearest: ICE neither required the alleged compulsion nor capped compensation at $1. *Nwauzor* likewise rejected federal defenses to state wage enforcement at a private facility. This suggests a document-first contract audit should isolate:

- mandatory versus discretionary PBNDS language;
- reimbursement formulas versus wage ceilings;
- contractor-created handbooks, sanitation rules, and disciplinary schedules;
- staffing proposals showing which paid positions detainee labor substitutes for; and
- correspondence showing whether ICE approved, knew of, or rejected deviations.

### 2. A $1 reimbursement rate is not necessarily a lawful compensation ceiling

Across *Menocal*, *Nwauzor*, and *Novoa*, GEO has defended programs built around nominal daily payments. The *Menocal* order is particularly useful because it separates the reimbursement amount from the contractor's power to pay more. Future contract tracing should therefore avoid treating an invoice or reimbursement line as proof that GEO was forbidden to pay higher wages.

### 3. Litigation exposure persists after appellate procedural wins and losses

The 2026 Supreme Court *Menocal* ruling resolved only interlocutory appealability. The *Nwauzor* judgment is subject to a still-pending certiorari petition. *Novoa* remains stayed rather than adjudicated. *Reid* is active on state-law claims despite dismissal of the federal count. A status field that says “terminated” or “appeal decided” is therefore insufficient without reading the operative order and subsequent docket activity.

### 4. Allegation/holding discipline materially changes the story

The complaint allegations in *Novoa* and *Reid* are serious, but they have not all been adjudicated. *Bilal* revived claims under the motion-to-dismiss standard and later ended by stipulation. *Pesci* produced a defense judgment. *Newsome* produced a defense judgment and was initially a topical false positive. The matrix accompanying this report separates each category explicitly.
