## Bottom line

Roycroft is a genuine False Claims Act case about Ohio Medicaid billing at a residential drug-and-alcohol treatment center in Shelby, Ohio. It is **not** an ICE, DHS, detention, or federal procurement-contract case. The relevant payment stream was provider claims to Ohio Medicaid; the reviewed record identifies no federal contract, procurement vehicle, award number, ICE facility, or DHS component.

The case also does **not** establish that GEO or a Cornell entity committed billing fraud. Relator J. Lynn Roycroft alleged that group counseling was billed when not provided and that Medicaid claims impliedly certified compliance with service-hour, documentation, and staff-qualification requirements. The Sixth Circuit stated that six of seven identified claims were in fact billed to Medicaid and that presentment was not disputed. It nevertheless held that the complaint failed to identify what was false in any representative claim or connect a specific claim to a specific facet of the alleged scheme. The court affirmed dismissal with prejudice under Rule 9(b). That is an adjudication of pleading sufficiency, not an adjudication that the alleged services were or were not delivered.

The United States declined to intervene. That procedural choice is recorded accurately but is not treated here as approval, disapproval, or an adjudication of Roycroft's allegations.

A material attribution caveat emerged from the SEC record. Roycroft's alleged knowledge and conduct period was May 2008 through March 2009. GEO did not complete its acquisition of Cornell Companies until August 12, 2010. The appellate opinion uses “Geo Group” collectively for the defendants and says the defendants operated the center, but the caption and shorthand do not, by themselves, prove parent-level GEO operational responsibility during the earlier alleged period. The full complaints and corporate-successor allegations are unavailable in the reviewed public record and should be obtained before assigning pre-acquisition conduct directly to the GEO parent.

## Alleged billing scheme

The Sixth Circuit characterized the first amended complaint as advancing two principal theories.

First, Roycroft alleged billing for group counseling not provided. Morning, evening, and closure groups plus moral inventory were allegedly bundled into a three-hour daily unit for each adolescent. Roycroft alleged that evening group was infrequent or partial, closure group historically was not provided because the schedule lacked time, and some counseling blocks were used for snack time, chores, or television.

Second, Roycroft alleged implied false certification. She asserted that payment codes and identifiers conveyed compliance without disclosing alleged failures to:

1. provide the required twenty weekly hours of alcohol and drug addiction services;
2. document group counseling properly; and
3. use qualified personnel for group sessions.

These remain allegations. The appellate court reviewed them under a motion-to-dismiss standard; it did not find that the underlying conduct occurred.

## Representative claims: what was established and what was not

The amended complaint identified seven claims as representative of the alleged scheme. The appellate opinion quotes one example: a Medicaid claim for counseling services attributed to Rebecca A. Wagers, CDCA, for client C.R. on September 20, 2008.

The key evidentiary distinction is unusually sharp:

- **Presentment:** The actual invoices were attached to defendants' reply. The Sixth Circuit stated that six of seven claims were billed to Medicaid and that GEO did not dispute presentment.
- **Falsity:** The court could not determine what was false in any representative claim. The complaint did not identify whether a specific billed claim involved morning, evening, or closure group; which treatment, staffing, licensing, or documentation violation applied; what portion of a service was not delivered; or how much a claim was inflated.
- **Timing:** Some allegations concerned knowledge or conduct months after the September 2008 claims. The opinion specifically noted that Roycroft said she learned in February 2009 that evening group was infrequent or partial, without alleging that the same was true when the representative claims were submitted in September 2008.

The court therefore affirmed failure of the presentment claim under 31 U.S.C. § 3729(a)(1)(A). It also affirmed failure of the reverse-false-claims/overpayment theory under § 3729(a)(1)(G) because Roycroft conceded that theory depended on the presentment claim. The panel did not consider a separate § 3729(a)(1)(B) false-records theory because the appellate argument was premised on presentment rather than a distinct false record or statement.

## Corporate attribution and acquisition timing

Cornell Companies' August 18, 2010 Form 8-K reports that GEO completed the Cornell acquisition on August 12, 2010 and that Cornell became a wholly owned GEO subsidiary. The transaction included GEO stock and about $85 million cash, and GEO assumed about $175.4 million of Cornell debt.

That closing postdates Roycroft's May 2008–March 2009 employment and alleged billing period. The timing does not remove later-acquired Cornell entities or GEO from the lawsuit, resolve successor liability, or establish which legal entity submitted each Medicaid claim. It does require disciplined language:

- safe: “Roycroft alleged misconduct at a Cornell Abraxas Ohio facility; GEO and Cornell entities were defendants after GEO's 2010 acquisition”;
- unsafe on this record: “GEO parent management directed the 2008–09 billing” or “GEO itself submitted the claims during Roycroft's employment.”

The unavailable complaints should be obtained to determine the pleaded corporate-liability and successor theories.

## Tier 2 / procurement-fraud eligibility effect

Roycroft should be included in the broad GEO litigation map as an **independent healthcare-billing allegation and pleading-standard outcome**. It should **not** be counted as an ICE/DHS contract context, a federal procurement-fraud case, an adjudicated fraud violation, a government enforcement success, or an adverse merits finding that GEO submitted false claims.

For any comparative procurement-fraud synthesis, this case is allegation/procedural only. The court reached whether the relator pleaded falsity with particularity; it did not decide whether the identified Medicaid claims were actually false. The most probative adjudicated point is the distinction between undisputed presentment of six claims and the unproven/insufficiently specified falsity of those claims.

## Learnings

- [Methodology] An appellate court can authenticate that claims were submitted while simultaneously finding the complaint insufficient to allege why they were false. Those propositions must not be collapsed.
- [Methodology] A later parent-company acquisition creates a serious date-sensitive attribution issue; a collective appellate shorthand is not enough to assign pre-acquisition operating conduct to the parent.
- [Methodology] Medicaid provider billing is government payment activity but not necessarily procurement or a contract award. It should remain outside the ICE/DHS contract universe unless separate evidence supplies that link.
- [Methodology] Declination of intervention is a procedural position without a disclosed rationale in the available record.
- [Source quality] CourtListener provided the complete docket skeleton, but GovInfo supplied the authenticated full opinion; PACER/Clerk retrieval remains necessary for the underlying pleadings, invoices, and district order.
