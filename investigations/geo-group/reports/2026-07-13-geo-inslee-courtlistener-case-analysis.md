## Bottom line

The earlier appellate story is complete, but the litigation is not. In March 2024, the district court preliminarily enjoined Washington from enforcing Sections 2, 3, 5, and 6 of HB 1470 against GEO at the Northwest ICE Processing Center (NWIPC). The Ninth Circuit vacated that injunction in August 2025. Its central holding was that the proper comparators for an intergovernmental-immunity discrimination analysis are Washington private civil-detention settings—not prisons and jails. It also held that Section 2 did not directly regulate the federal government, rejected field and obstacle preemption as to Sections 2, 3, and 6, and held that the Governor and Attorney General were not proper defendants for the private cause of action in Section 5. Rehearing was denied in a published February 2026 order and the mandate issued March 4, 2026.

On remand, the district court found the current Chapter 70.395 burdens substantially similar to those applied to residential treatment and involuntary civil-commitment facilities. On July 9, 2026, it entered a new, limited preliminary injunction **against GEO**, requiring GEO to admit Washington Department of Health (DOH) inspectors to the Tacoma facility while excluding ICE-controlled administrative and medical areas and prohibiting review or copying of ICE records. The order was stayed for 14 days. GEO filed a notice of interlocutory appeal on July 13, 2026. As of the research cutoff, CourtListener does not display the new Ninth Circuit number. The district docket remains open.

That current posture matters for the ICE-contract investigation. GEO's 2026 filing says it and ICE signed a new contract on March 27, 2026 for detention, transportation, and food services supporting ICE ERO Seattle. That description is a **party representation**, not an independently verified contract term. The underlying contract exhibit is docketed but not available through RECAP. The July 9 court nevertheless found that GEO controls ingress and egress to the detainee-facing portions of the facility and carved out the ICE-controlled administrative and medical areas from the inspection injunction.

## Key Discoveries

- The Ninth Circuit vacated the 2024 injunction, rejected GEO's direct-regulation and preemption arguments, and required private civil-detention comparators rather than prisons and jails.
- A new July 9, 2026 injunction runs against GEO and permits limited DOH inspection while protecting ICE-controlled administrative and medical areas and ICE records.
- GEO appealed on July 13; the public CourtListener snapshot has no new Ninth Circuit number yet.
- GEO says a replacement ICE contract was signed March 27, 2026, but the actual contract exhibit is not in RECAP and must be obtained before its PIID, value, term, and control clauses can be treated as verified.
- Inslee, the parallel DOH access case, and Nwauzor overlap at NWIPC and in contractor doctrine but assert distinct statutory claims and should remain separate matters.

## Parties, facility, and ICE contract interests

### Parties and roles

- **Plaintiff / counterclaim defendant / current appellant:** The GEO Group, Inc., a Florida corporation headquartered in Boca Raton.
- **Original defendants:** Washington Governor Jay Inslee and Attorney General Robert W. Ferguson, sued in their official capacities.
- **Current official-capacity substitutions reflected in the July 2026 notice:** Governor Robert W. Ferguson and Attorney General Nicholas W. Brown.
- **District judge:** Benjamin H. Settle, W.D. Washington.
- **2025 Ninth Circuit panel:** Judges William A. Fletcher, Ronald M. Gould, and Jacqueline H. Nguyen.
- **United States amicus:** Supported GEO only on the intergovernmental-immunity discrimination issue; it expressly did not address GEO's other theories. Its position was that prisons and jails were the correct comparators. The panel rejected that comparator.
- **Other amici:** Washington DOH and Department of Labor and Industries; La Resistencia and Professor Angelina Snodgrass Godoy; Northwest Immigrant Rights Project; and Immigration Reform Law Institute.

### NWIPC and contract chronology

The complaint identifies NWIPC at 1623 East J Street, Tacoma, Washington, and alleges a 1,575-bed capacity. According to the complaint, Corrections Services Corporation opened the facility in April 2004 under federal contract ACL-2-C-004; GEO acquired that company, the facility, and the contract in 2005 and expanded the site. Those historical acquisition and capacity details are **GEO allegations in its pleading**, not factual findings made after trial.

The complaint describes ICE contract HSCEDM-15-D-00015 as signed September 24, 2015 and effective September 28, 2015, initially with a one-year base period and nine one-year options plus a half-year option. It says a January 29, 2021 modification removed the remaining options and created a September 28, 2020–September 27, 2025 performance period. The complaint alleged that state-law compliance could cause more than $160 million in lost revenue and more than $3 million in HVAC and building costs. These dollar amounts remain **GEO's claimed injuries**, not adjudicated damages.

The 2015 contract incorporated ICE's 2011 Performance-Based National Detention Standards and required compliance with applicable federal, state, and local laws and standards. Both the district court and Ninth Circuit treated the contractual language requiring the most stringent applicable standard as important. The Ninth Circuit quoted the contract:

> “All services [at the NWIPC] must comply with . . . all applicable federal, state, and local laws and standards. Should a conflict exist between any of these standards, the most stringent shall apply.”

In a June 9, 2026 motion, GEO represented that it and ICE entered a new contract on March 27, 2026 for detention, transportation, and food services at NWIPC supporting ICE ERO Seattle. GEO cited an “ICE/ERO Control Over Facility Access” clause and argued it required preclearance for access to ICE staff, facilities, and information. The court did not accept GEO's broader proposition that it lacked access control. The court found GEO controlled entry to housing units, courtrooms, holding rooms, and the day-to-day areas it operates, while recognizing ICE control of administrative areas and uncertainty or federal control in medical areas. The current contract exhibit is not in RECAP, so its PIID, ceiling, period of performance, complete access clause, and modification history remain unverified in this case pass.

## Claims in the 2023 complaint

GEO sought declaratory, preliminary, and permanent injunctive relief under 28 U.S.C. §§ 2201–2202 and 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Its five counts were:

1. intergovernmental immunity—direct regulation;
2. intergovernmental immunity—discriminatory regulation;
3. field preemption;
4. conflict/obstacle preemption; and
5. Contracts Clause.

The complaint challenged Sections 2 through 6 of HB 1470, later codified in Chapter 70.395 RCW. In broad terms, those provisions imposed health and safety requirements, inspections, operational standards, a private cause of action, and civil penalties on covered private detention facilities. The complaint's constitutional and preemption contentions are **GEO positions**; the holdings are set out separately below.

## Disposition and holdings

### March 8, 2024 district order

The district court's initial preliminary-injunction order, reported at 720 F. Supp. 3d 1029, held:

- The then-current challenge to Section 4 / RCW 70.395.060 was unripe because the existing contract ran through September 27, 2025 and a replacement or modification was speculative. The court dismissed those claims without prejudice but without leave to amend and denied preliminary relief as to Section 4.
- Challenges to Sections 2, 3, 5, and 6 were ripe.
- Using Washington prisons and jails as comparators, the court initially held that Sections 2, 3, 5, and 6 discriminatorily burdened GEO in violation of intergovernmental immunity and preliminarily enjoined their enforcement against GEO as operator of NWIPC.
- GEO's alternative direct-regulation, field-preemption, conflict-preemption, and Contracts Clause theories directed at Section 2 were dismissed with prejudice.
- The court rejected GEO's characterization of ICE's PBNDS as congressional law that displaced applicable state requirements.

The April 15, 2024 reconsideration order denied the State's motion, clarified that DOH and L&I were bound by Rule 65(d)(2), and maintained the prison/jail comparator. It acknowledged that the Governor and Attorney General lacked authority to enforce the private right of action in Section 5. The Ninth Circuit later vacated the injunction and rejected the comparator analysis.

### August 19, 2025 Ninth Circuit merits opinion

The corrected published decision, 151 F.4th 1107, is the controlling appellate merits decision. It held:

- HB 1232's 2025 amendments did not moot the appeal because the operative scheme remained substantially similar.
- Section 2 did **not** directly regulate the federal government. NWIPC is privately owned and operated by GEO; the statute did not prohibit the facility, eliminate ICE's use of a contractor, or stop federal detention.
- The correct comparators for discriminatory-regulation analysis are private civil-detention facilities, particularly residential treatment and involuntary civil-commitment facilities—not state prisons and jails. The court remanded the burden comparison to the district court.
- The Section 6 penalty scheme, as amended, also required comparison on remand.
- Sections 2, 3, and 6 were not field-preempted and did not create obstacle preemption. State health and safety regulation sits within historic state police powers, Congress had not clearly occupied the field, and GEO's ICE contract contemplated more stringent state requirements.
- The Governor and Attorney General were not proper defendants for Section 5's privately enforced cause of action. The district court should have dismissed that portion of GEO's suit instead of reaching its merits.

The exact disposition was:

> “We vacate the district court’s grant of a preliminary injunction against Sections 2, 3, 5 and 6 of HB 1470. We grant in part the motion to remand (Dkt. No. 63) to the district court for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. VACATED and REMANDED.”

### Rehearing and mandate

GEO sought panel and en banc rehearing. On February 11, 2026, the panel unanimously denied panel rehearing and the full court failed to provide a majority for en banc rehearing. The published order includes a panel statement defending the civil-detention comparator and a dissent by Judge Patrick Bumatay, joined by Judges Callahan, Bennett, R. Nelson, Collins, Bress, VanDyke, and Tung, arguing that prisons and jails were the correct comparators. The dissent is not the holding.

The Ninth Circuit denied GEO's motion to stay the mandate on February 24, and the mandate issued March 4, 2026, making the August 2025 judgment effective.

## Remand and the July 2026 injunction

After mandate, Washington obtained leave to assert a counterclaim under RCW 70.395.050 and moved for a preliminary injunction. Its motion alleged attempted inspections under separate and overlapping authorities, including a September 3, 2025 attempt under RCW 43.70.170 and March 20 and April 20, 2026 attempts under RCW 70.395.050. Those inspection-history details are **State allegations** in its motion.

The July 9, 2026 order held, for preliminary-injunction purposes:

- Washington had standing.
- ICE was not a necessary and indispensable party; the public-right exception supplied an alternative basis.
- GEO did not obtain Yearsley derivative sovereign immunity because it did not identify congressionally authorized federal direction to deny state inspection.
- A federal procurement contract alone cannot preempt state law.
- On the Ninth Circuit's comparator framework, Chapter 70.395 imposed burdens substantially similar to those applied to residential treatment and involuntary civil-commitment facilities and was not discriminatory.
- GEO's motion to dismiss the State counterclaim and its sanctions motion were denied.
- Potential contract consequences did not defeat irreparable-harm analysis because a court injunction would control over a potential contractual breach.

The operative relief is narrower than Washington requested:

> “The GEO Group shall admit Washington Department of Health inspectors to the Tacoma facility to enforce RCW 70.395.050(2), except with respect to ICE-controlled administrative and medical areas.”

GEO may decide whether its employees accompany inspectors. Inspection does not authorize review or copying of ICE records; DOH must seek additional relief for specific documents. The injunction is directed to GEO, and the court warned that sanctions could follow disobedience. GEO had to notify all Tacoma-facility employees within 48 hours. The court stayed the injunction for 14 days to permit appeal.

On July 13, 2026, GEO filed a notice stating:

> “This appeal is a preliminary injunction appeal within the meaning of Ninth Circuit Rule 3-3 and is taken pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1).”

CourtListener docket 67606656 remains open and lists July 13 as the latest filing. No new Ninth Circuit docket number is displayed in the public record at the research cutoff.

## Current statute and rulemaking status

HB 1232 took effect May 12, 2025. Among other changes, it removed the “for-profit” limitation from the covered-facility definition and amended inspection, publication, civil-fine, and exclusion provisions. Current Chapter 70.395 includes:

- RCW 70.395.020: covered private detention facility definition;
- RCW 70.395.040: health and safety requirements;
- RCW 70.395.050: DOH inspection authority;
- RCW 70.395.055: DOH civil fines, including up to $10,000 per violation and an aggregate ceiling of $1 million in specified repeat/noncorrection circumstances;
- RCW 70.395.058: publication requirements;
- RCW 70.395.060: operational requirements;
- RCW 70.395.070: private right of action;
- RCW 70.395.080: civil penalties; and
- RCW 70.395.100: exclusions.

DOH's rulemaking page still describes Chapter 246-385 WAC as draft rule language. It records a November 13, 2023 CR-101 and a second CR-101 filed September 15, 2025 for juvenile facilities after HB 1232. A second informal public-comment period ended July 7, 2026. The published schedule calls the formal comment period and public hearing **tentative** for summer 2026. This supports only a bounded conclusion: as of the page captured July 13, the rules remained in draft/informal-comment status; it does not prove that no later agency filing exists outside that page.

## Related proceedings: connected but not merged

### State of Washington, Department of Health v. GEO

Washington DOH filed Thurston County No. 23-2-04215-34 on December 19, 2023 seeking facility access under HB 1470. It passed through W.D. Wash. No. 3:24-cv-05029 and was later removed/refiled as W.D. Wash. No. 3:24-cv-05639, CourtListener docket 69016834. On July 9, 2026, the court entered a parallel limited injunction under the general inspection authority in RCW 43.70.170, with the same ICE-controlled administrative and medical carveout and a 14-day stay. GEO filed a notice of appeal on July 13. The public snapshot likewise does not show the new Ninth Circuit number. This proceeding is closely related but distinct: its authority and procedural vehicle differ from the Chapter 70.395 counterclaim in Inslee.

### Nwauzor v. GEO

Nwauzor concerns compensation for detainee work at NWIPC under Washington minimum-wage law. Inslee concerns health, safety, and inspection regulation under Chapter 70.395. The cases intersect at the same facility and in federal-contractor/Supremacy Clause doctrine, but they must not be merged as one controversy. The Supreme Court docket in Nwauzor, No. 25-828, shows a January 9, 2026 certiorari petition and a May 18, 2026 order inviting the Solicitor General to file a brief. It remained pending at the cutoff.

### Labor and Industries access case

W.D. Wash. No. 3:24-cv-05095 ended in an October 2024 stipulated permanent access arrangement under RCW 49.17.070. The July 2026 Inslee order cited this history when rejecting GEO's assertion that it was powerless to facilitate inspection. It is useful comparison evidence, not the same claim.

## Learnings

- [Source quality] CourtListener/RECAP supplied the complaint, all four opinion texts, GEO and U.S. appellate briefs, remand motions, mandate, July injunction, and appeal notice, but not the new ICE contract exhibit or the State's principal appellate briefs.
- [Methodology] Treat docket metadata, party allegations, amicus positions, judicial holdings, and operative relief as separate evidence classes; this prevented GEO's contract-access theory from being mistaken for the court's holding.
- [Process gap] A newly filed notice of appeal can precede public assignment of an appellate docket number, so follow-up needs to pivot on the district notice and party names rather than assume nonfiling.
