## Page-by-page review

### Page 1

The caption names Stearns Brattle Street, LLC and others as plaintiffs and Hicham Ali Hassan and others as defendants. Footnote 1 names the other landlord plaintiffs as Marshall W. Stearns Trust, LLC and Brattle Square Properties, LLC. Footnote 2 names the additional defendants as Paloma Tannery, LLC and Eastern Bank, Citizens Bank, Santander Bank, and Bank of America, as trustees.

The introduction describes Stearns Brattle and the two related entities collectively as the landlords, Paloma Tannery, LLC as their former tenant, and Hicham Ali Hassan as Paloma's personal guarantor. It says, after a November 7, 2018 hearing, “Stearns Brattle's motion is ALLOWED.”

The court says that, at the relevant times, Stearns Brattle owned the real estate at 37, 39, 39A, and 41A Brattle Street in Cambridge and that Hassan owned Nader Corp. and Paloma. Nader Corp. leased the property on February 8, 2007, with Hassan signing as guarantor. Nader assigned the lease to Paloma on June 1, 2011, and Hassan signed the assignment for both companies.

### Page 2

The assignment did not release Nader Corp. from the lease. The parties amended the lease on August 29, 2012, extending it through May 31, 2017; Hassan signed for Paloma and as guarantor. The court says Hassan operated a retail store at the property until July 2016 and Colliers International managed the property for Stearns Brattle.

The court says the store was chained and locked during July 2016. Hassan posted a sign that said the store had experienced water damage, a statement the court says he later admitted was untrue. The court states: “In reality, Hassan closed the business for financial reasons.” Paloma did not pay its $52,138.15 monthly rent in August 2016.

By an August 15, 2016 termination letter, landlord counsel John Thompson told the defendants that the lease was terminated and that Paloma and Hassan, as guarantor, remained jointly and severally liable for rent for the remaining lease term, along with damages, costs and attorneys' fees. Defense counsel Michael Morizio responded on August 29, saying his clients had surrendered the lease and had “quit and delivered legal and physical possession of the Premises to the Lessor.” On August 31, Colliers executive vice president Dennis Callahan and others inspected the premises with Morizio.

### Page 3

Morizio gave Paloma's keys to Callahan and later said Callahan accepted the surrender. Callahan denied agreeing that rent or other sums would be forgiven or waived and said he lacked authority to waive rent or agree to a surrender.

The court then states: “Also in August 2016, Colliers suggested to Hassan's nephew that he consider renting the property for his retail business. On September 1, 2016, the nephew's company, ALAIA, LLC (‘ALAIA’), and Stearns Brattle executed a ‘Tenancy at Will Agreement’ for the rental of the property for $30,000 a month. ALAIA continued to rent the property through May 2017, the month the defendant's lease was set to expire.” The opinion does not name the nephew and therefore does not resolve which nephew controlled ALAIA.

The court states: “On July 10, 2017, Stearns Brattle filed its complaint seeking unpaid rent in the amount of $267,356.86. According to Callahan, that amount represents the difference between the amount of rent Paloma owed for the period between August 2016 and May 2017 and the rent ALAIA paid for those months.” This is an amount sought in the complaint; the memorandum does not state a separately liquidated final judgment amount.

### Page 4

The court quotes lease sections 7.1, 7.2 and 8.11. Section 7.1 provided that reentry or resumed possession would not itself accept a surrender or waive the landlord's rights, and required the tenant to indemnify the landlord for rent loss after termination for the remainder of the term. Section 7.2 allowed the landlord to recover reasonable expenditures and attorneys' fees after a default. Section 8.11 required amendments and waivers to be in a writing signed by both landlord and tenant.

### Page 5

The court states: “The parties do not dispute that the terms of the lease unambiguously provide that Stearns Brattle is entitled to rent for the unpaid months of August 2016 through May 2017, and costs and attorney's fees, minus the amount it collected from ALAIA through reasonable mitigation for the benefit of the defendants. They also do not dispute that Hassan is personally liable under the lease as guarantor.”

The defendants nevertheless argued that the landlords accepted a surrender through Callahan, that the termination notice was defective, and that the landlord waived additional lease rights.

### Pages 6–7

The court rejected surrender and waiver. It reasoned that the termination letter expressly reserved the landlords' right to remaining rent; Morizio's account of Callahan saying “yes” to a surrender did not establish a clear intention to release the tenant from payment obligations. The lease's signed-writing requirement had not been waived. The court also found no evidence that Stearns Brattle gave Colliers actual or apparent authority to amend the lease or bind the landlords contractually. The opinion says Callahan denied that authority and Morizio could not identify the broker's name or employer at the inspection.

Footnote 4 says Thompson's August 29, 2016 email noting that the premises had been “officially surrendered” did not prove an intention to release the defendants, particularly because the landlord reserved its rights.

### Page 8

The court says the defendants supplied no evidence contradicting the reasonableness of the $30,000 monthly ALAIA rent, considering unsuccessful attempts to rent to other retailers and the short vacancy. It rejected the defective-notice defense because the defendants had already vacated and did not seek to cure; the court deemed any deficiency in the contractual ten-day notice immaterial in those circumstances.

The disposition states: “For all of these reasons, Stearns Brattle's motion for summary judgment is ALLOWED.” The order repeats that the plaintiffs' motion is allowed. It is signed by Janice W. Howe and dated December 4, 2018.

## Evidentiary limits

The memorandum adjudicates the summary-judgment issues described above and expressly records the parties' non-dispute over the guaranty and lease entitlement. The $267,356.86 figure is the complaint's requested unpaid-rent amount as described by the court. This eight-page memorandum does not state a final liquidated judgment amount, post-judgment interest, later costs, payment, satisfaction, execution, appeal, or current balance. The reference to “Hassan's nephew” and “the nephew's company” is the court's description; it does not identify Talal, Tarek, or another person by name.
