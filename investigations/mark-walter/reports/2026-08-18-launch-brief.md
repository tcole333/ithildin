## Publication-safe assessment

1. Delaware Life Insurance Company (DLIC) disclosed that DLIC and Clear Spring Life and Annuity Company (CSLAC) received Southern District of New York grand-jury subpoenas in February 2026 and that the SEC is conducting a parallel investigation. DLIC said it understood the inquiry to concern classification of certain private-credit investments as affiliated or related-party and said it was cooperating. The filing does not say Mark Walter personally is a target, identify a charged offense, or establish fraud. See finding #14733 and the [amended SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/704843/000119312526286687/d51935dnvpfsa.htm).

2. A material disclosure correction is established. DLIC's original 2025 tables disclosed approximately USD 1.290 billion of affiliated and related-party investments; the corrected relevant categories total approximately USD 17.878 billion. Corrected 2024 comparison categories rose from approximately USD 2.262 billion to USD 11.559 billion. The comparable June 2026 category total was approximately USD 18.670 billion. The amount, correction, and concentration are facts; intent, loss, and final legal or accounting classification are unresolved. Findings #14732 and #14734–#14736.

3. Public records show more than anonymous third-party exposure. DBRS described Whitney Funding as a bankruptcy-remote vehicle established by DLIC, with DLIC serving at closing as sole lender, managing lender, manager, and servicer. An executed 2022 credit agreement defined Whitney and Reservoir as “DLIC Controlled Funds.” DLIC's schedules show loan sales to Whitney while DLIC continued to hold or fund Whitney note classes. This establishes functional control and continuing pool exposure, but not beneficial ownership, loan-level round trips, recourse, Walter as an end borrower, or fraud. Findings #14816 and #14818–#14822; [DBRS release](https://dbrs.morningstar.com/research/407470/dbrs-morningstar-assigns-and-upgrades-provisional-ratings-on-the-loans-issued-by-whitney-funding-llc).

4. Liquidity evidence is mixed. DLIC reported approximately USD 7.804 billion of cash and short-term investments and approximately USD 4.028 billion of surplus at June 2026, while FHLB funding agreements rose to approximately USD 4.963 billion and first-half surrenders increased. AM Best affirmed A- ratings but changed outlooks to negative after the reclassification. DLIC did not express going-concern doubt, and its 2025 filing said certain sales generating negative interest-maintenance-reserve effects were not compelled by liquidity. These are signs of active liquidity and capital management, not proof of insolvency or a regulator-forced asset sale. Findings #14745–#14750.

5. The 2026 Lakers transaction is an agreement, not a completed NBA-approved transfer as of the cutoff. The reported USD 10 billion and USD 12.5 billion figures are franchise valuations, not disclosed purchase price, Walter proceeds, or profit. A liquidity motive is plausible but remains an inference because no public sale agreement, financing commitment, proceeds waterfall, collateral release, or regulator order has been located. Findings #14762–#14767; [NBA/Lakers 2025 closing announcement](https://www.nba.com/lakers/news/mark-walter-acquires-majority-stake-in-lakers) and [AP's 2026 agreement report](https://apnews.com/article/lakers-sale-kushner-iger-12-billion-d5058bb845d87873e23e270a6bb9be9f).

6. Walter-linked nonprofits disclose substantial insider debt, but their balance sheets do not establish Walter's personal liquidity. The Walter Family Foundation reported USD 39.313 million of aggregate officer loans as all of its 2024 liabilities; the filing does not allocate the current balance among all note entries. White Oak Conservation Foundation separately reported USD 99.110 million due to Walter and USD 6.100 million due to DLHPII Good Works; Chicago Beyond reported USD 91.611 million and USD 6.000 million respectively. Only White Oak's filing expressly described the relevant notes as zero-interest and unsecured. Findings #14743 and #14755–#14757.

7. The Los Angeles Dodgers Foundation disclosed a USD 974,796 administrative-services transaction with the Dodgers and five owner-directors. Its bylaws gave Walter appointment and removal power over directors and required his or a designee's presence for board business. These are concrete governance and related-party facts, not evidence that pricing was unfair or authority was misused. Findings #14793–#14794.

8. White Oak's USD 105.210 million officer-loan balance is cumulative, not comparable to one year's operating expense. From the beginning of 2016 through 2024, officer loans rose USD 104.371 million while assets rose USD 51.059 million and net assets declined USD 53.500 million. Public records identify Holdings as the campus landholder and mixed-use conservation/hospitality operator, while the Foundation reports approximately USD 55.282 million on the Form 990-PF investment-purpose land/building/equipment line. No reviewed public record explains that allocation through a lease, reimbursement arrangement, or fixed-asset schedule. White Oak is closed to ordinary tours and events, not operationally shut down; golf is contextual rather than an independent theory. Findings #14835–#14855 and the [White Oak public-record deep dive](./2026-08-19-white-oak-deep-dive.md).
