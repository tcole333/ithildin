# White Oak Conservation: public-record deep dive

**Cutoff:** August 19, 2026  
**Profile:** `mark-walter`  
**Entities kept separate:** White Oak Conservation Foundation Inc. (the nonprofit/private operating foundation) and White Oak Conservation Holdings LLC (the taxable property and operating company)

## Executive assessment

The officer-loan balance is large and worth understanding, but it is not comparable to one year's operating expense. The Foundation's 2024 Form 990-PF reported USD 105.210 million of loans from officers against USD 55.957 million of assets. The debt accumulated over roughly a decade while the organization both ran recurring operating deficits and built a substantial fixed-asset base. In 2024 alone, the Foundation's USD 11.915 million deficit and USD 0.363 million asset increase reconcile exactly to its USD 12.278 million increase in total liabilities. This is an accounting bridge, not proof that every advance can be traced to a particular payment.

The public-facing closure is narrower than an operational shutdown. White Oak says it is not open for tours and public events while it expands wildlife and education programs and renovates the property. Current wildlife programs, training, employment, water-system records, construction activity, and regulatory filings show continuing operations.

The golf and retreat operation is real, but nothing found makes it independently newsworthy. Its limited relevance is structural: the same Holdings entity and campus encompass regulated conservation work, titled land, and private mixed-use amenities, while the Foundation reports the charitable spending. Current employment records include golf roles alongside wildlife, veterinary, conservation-recovery, maintenance, hospitality, and estate roles; internal-candidate duplicates inflate the raw job count.

The most consequential unresolved public-record question is an entity and asset-allocation question: the Foundation reports approximately USD 55.282 million on the Form 990-PF land/building/equipment investment line, while the principal White Oak campus parcels reviewed to date are titled to Holdings. Public filings reviewed so far do not disclose a lease, cost-sharing agreement, or other arrangement that reconciles those two facts. That absence does not establish that no agreement exists; an unrecorded lease or internal allocation would ordinarily not be public. Public permits, recorded instruments, property cards, and annual tax filings can still narrow what the Foundation appears to have funded and which entity owns or applied for each project.

From 2017 through 2024, reported revenue totaled approximately USD 13.851 million and expenses approximately USD 63.160 million, producing cumulative annual deficits of approximately USD 49.309 million. Over the same span, assets rose by approximately USD 39.657 million and liabilities by approximately USD 88.498 million. The comparison explains why a cumulative loan balance can greatly exceed a single year's expense: advances supported both years of negative operating results and asset construction or accumulation. It does not identify the payer or recipient of each dollar.

The fuller 2016-beginning to 2024-ending balance-sheet bridge is exact. Officer loans rose from USD 0.839 million to USD 105.210 million, a net increase of USD 104.371 million. Total liabilities rose USD 104.559 million; assets rose USD 51.059 million; and net assets declined USD 53.500 million. Thus USD 51.059 million of asset growth equals the USD 104.559 million liability increase less the USD 53.500 million depletion of net assets. Annual reported deficits over those years totaled USD 51.233 million, while three separately reported "other decreases" to net assets totaled USD 2.268 million. Again, these are year-end balances and net changes, not gross cash advances or proof of loan forgiveness or repayment.

Within assets, gross land/building/equipment basis rose by USD 2,507,837, accumulated depreciation rose by USD 2,730,365, net fixed assets fell by USD 222,528, cash rose by USD 585,993, and savings fell by USD 360.

The officer-loan schedule attributes USD 99,110,173 to Mark Walter and USD 6,100,000 to DLHPII Good Works LLC. It describes both as zero-interest, unsecured, payable on demand, and used "to fund operating activities." The Walter row gives an original amount of USD 839,000 but a much larger current balance, consistent with additional advances under or alongside the listed note, although the public return does not explain the mechanics. The DLHPII row contains an apparent date anomaly: a January 2024 note date and June 2023 maturity date despite also saying the loan is on demand.

### What the Foundation says it spent money on

The 2024 return classified USD 8.842 million as direct charitable wildlife-service activity and USD 0.286 million as conservation-education activity. Major disclosed expenses included USD 4.022 million of employee wages, USD 0.961 million of benefits, USD 2.730 million of depreciation, USD 1.549 million of animal feed and supplements, and USD 0.839 million of animal-habitat repair and maintenance.

The five highest-paid contractors were described as providing metal-fence coating, new habitat fencing, habitat repair and maintenance, and habitat expansion or improvement, totaling approximately USD 1.866 million. Those descriptions are meaningful counterevidence to a theory that the nonprofit's reported spending is simply golf spending, although they do not independently establish how every capital addition was allocated.

This is primarily an operating foundation, not a conventional grantmaking foundation. The 2018–2023 returns disclose only six outbound conservation grants totaling USD 63,451, for okapi, Sumatran tiger, Asian elephant, and Sumatra conservation-response projects; the 2024 return reports no grants. That small grant total should not be mistaken for the scale of its direct wildlife operations.

A donor-side search identified a lower-bound set of 14 grants totaling USD 667,567 to White Oak across donor fiscal periods ending 2019–2023. The largest was USD 322,342 from the American Association of Zoological Parks and Aquariums for endangered-species COVID relief; International Crane Foundation rows totaled USD 124,981 and Fidelity Charitable rows USD 87,175. This does not reconcile to White Oak's contribution revenue because donor periods differ, some donor identities are redacted on the recipient return, and the local donor corpus is incomplete. It does show that outside conservation funding existed, but at a scale far below the officer financing.

## Entity, land, and mixed-use campus structure

Florida records keep the two principal entities distinct:

- White Oak Conservation Foundation Inc., EIN 46-4249512, is an active foreign nonprofit corporation and a federal private operating foundation. Mark and Kimbra Walter are directors.
- White Oak Conservation Holdings LLC, EIN 46-1966818, is an active foreign limited-liability company. Mark Walter is its manager.

Nassau County property records reviewed in this pass place the principal campus acreage in Holdings' name rather than the Foundation's. A current owner-name search returned 56 Holdings parcel rows, while the exact Foundation owner search returned none. The main parcel, 24-4N-25-0000-0001-0000, is 2,260.59 acres and includes conservation, timber, wetland, improvement, and 82.75 acres of golf-course coding. A separate 2025 tangible-personal-property account also names Holdings and reports approximately USD 7.912 million of market/assessed property. These are bounded county records, not a legal title opinion, but they materially sharpen the Foundation's USD 55.282 million line-11 classification question.

The county's White Oak Plantation overlay expressly allows conservation, research and education, golf, conference and entertainment space, lodging, staff residences, food and beverage, fitness, and related uses. The 2015 overlay text named both the Foundation and Holdings; a 2018 amendment revised the operative text to Holdings and increased the lodge cap from 120 to 150 rooms. The current 2045 plan names Holdings. Allowed use is not proof that every authorized facility has been built.

The Dye Course's own legal page identifies White Oak Conservation Holdings LLC as the site company. Its homepage calls the golf course part of a retreat "currently under development," says White Oak Conservation is a separate nonprofit, and represents that retreat profits will support the nonprofit. This is contextual evidence for a shared-campus model with separate legal entities; it does not by itself show actual profit transfers, improper benefit, or how common costs are allocated.

Holdings is not merely a golf or real-estate shell. A 2018 Army Corps public notice names Holdings as the applicant for expansion of the existing wildlife-conservation area and associated facilities. Federal animal and endangered-species records likewise name Holdings as the licensed or permitted conservation operator. The entity-allocation question therefore concerns a company that combines titled land, regulated conservation activity, and golf/retreat development, while the Foundation separately reports the charitable expenses and officer-financed asset base.

Additional public records reinforce that allocation without fully resolving it. Florida licensing records identify Holdings, doing business as White Oak Plantation, as the current vacation-rental and hotel/motel beverage licensee. A 2024 Nassau County notice of commencement names Holdings as owner for rehabilitation and expansion of an old lodge; the lender fields are blank, which is not proof that the project was self-funded. The Foundation separately holds a current health-care-clinic establishment permit at the shared address. In a 2026 deed, Nassau Conservation Holdings LLC transferred three parcels to Holdings and stated that both companies were wholly owned by the same **unnamed** owner and that beneficial ownership of the land did not change. The deed proves common ownership of those two companies but does not identify the owner or establish Foundation ownership.

The located secured-transaction records are narrow. Four exact Florida UCC hits for Holdings cover electric golf carts, Kubota tractors/loaders, and a Caterpillar track tractor/rake; three are lapsed or terminated and the Caterpillar filing was continued through 2027. Exact-name UCC searches found no Foundation or Nassau Conservation Holdings filing, and an exact Holdings-name Nassau mortgage search found no result. These bounded searches support identifiable equipment finance rather than a located land mortgage, but they do not exclude name variants, predecessor entities, parcel-indexed instruments, or unrecorded agreements.

A USD 577.39 Florida reemployment-tax lien recorded against the Foundation in 2021 was acknowledged paid and satisfied the following month. It is evidence that the Foundation functioned as an employer at the shared address, not evidence of continuing tax distress or Foundation land ownership.

The Foundation is separately the named recipient of USD 860,736 in U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service obligations from 2021 through 2025 for Florida grasshopper-sparrow propagation and reintroduction. No public award, permit, job record, or regulator filing reviewed in this pass says those funds or the officer-loan proceeds paid golf costs.

### Current jobs

Current job pages establish a mixed operating footprint, not a separate financial finding. The August 19 recruiting feed contained 37 rows, 17 of which were internal-candidate variants. Of the 20 outward-facing titles, five were coded as golf, five as conservation/animal care, three as hospitality/guest service, and seven as facilities/grounds/security. This is a posting snapshot, not employee headcount or evidence that every role was filled.

Historic job PDFs for turf and pool/spa roles expressly name Holdings as the employer. The current board uses the shared White Oak Conservation brand, so it cannot by itself establish which entity bears each present employee's payroll cost.

## Operating and regulatory status

### Not open to the public is not the same as shut down

White Oak's current support page says it is "not open for tours and public events" while it expands wildlife and conservation-education programs and renovates the property. Its contact page similarly says that, apart from education and career training, it is not open to the public to protect the animal environment. Archived pages show the tours/renovation language by October 2022, well before the current Walter investigation and Lakers questions. Google Maps displayed an undated "Temporarily closed" status in August 2026, but that label gives no effective date, reason, or scope. Current training, employment, animal-care, utility, and construction records are inconsistent with a facility-wide operational closure.

Independent records reinforce that conclusion. A Fish and Wildlife Service recovery report lists White Oak holding 23 whooping cranes at December 31, 2025; the Association of Zoos and Aquariums lists White Oak as an Accredited Related Facility through March 2029; and Florida wildlife officials identify it as a continuing partner in the grasshopper-sparrow release program.

### Florida drinking-water matter

Florida Department of Environmental Protection matter 24-1675 involved Holdings' public-water system, PWS 2454343. The file concerned missed monitoring or submissions for specified contaminants and periods, not a finding that the conservation nonprofit diverted funds. The consent package says remedial actions had already been completed and that acceptance was not an admission. A correction letter fixed numerical discrepancies in the original text. Holdings paid USD 4,600 on September 16, 2024—USD 4,350 in reduced civil penalty plus USD 250 in costs—and the October 31, 2024 closure letter says all consent-order conditions had been satisfied. The USD 5,850 figure appearing in a secondary violation database derives from superseded language and is not the final paid amount.

The FDEP portal contains continuing operating reports and testing into August 2026, further evidence that the water system and campus remain active. Any later corrective-action item must be evaluated separately rather than treated as continuation of the closed 2024 case.

In a separate February 2026 compliance-assistance matter, FDEP said a triennial TTHM sample due in June 2025 was collected in September 2025. The letter concerned late sampling; it did not say the result exceeded the contaminant limit. The attached December 2025 sanitary survey found the plant compliant and operating below design capacity, and a revised sampling plan followed. The public portal contains continuing records through August 2026 but no separately indexed closure letter or formal enforcement order for that assistance item, so its ultimate disposition is unresolved rather than proof of current adjudicated noncompliance.

### USDA animal-welfare warning

The underlying USDA APHIS inspection gives the warning important context. It reported that a 2.5-year-old female white rhinoceros died on May 29, 2024 after her head became trapped between enclosure rails. By the July 11 inspection, the facility had added extra rails. APHIS issued Holdings an official warning on December 5, case FL250033, under 9 C.F.R. 3.125(a); the notice expressly says it is neither final agency action nor an adjudicated finding. Later public records show zero non-compliant items in four routine inspections and two relicensing inspections through February 5, 2026, when Holdings' Class C exhibitor certificate was active and the inspection covered 383 animals across 27 species. This is a serious corrected animal-care incident, not evidence about the Foundation's loans or a continuing violation.

## Evidence boundaries

- The balance-sheet bridge explains the magnitude of liabilities; it does not prove the destination or propriety of each loan advance.
- Common ownership, branding, address, or staff does not collapse the Foundation and Holdings into one legal entity.
- A permit allowance is not proof of construction; a job advertisement is not proof a position was filled.
- A disclosed insider loan is not inherently misconduct, and nonprofit indebtedness is not evidence of Walter's personal liquidity.
- The title/fixed-asset mismatch is an unresolved accounting and allocation question, not a finding of private benefit or fraud.
